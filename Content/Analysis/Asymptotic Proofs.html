Asymptotic Proofs

Overview

In the previous section, we introduced asymptotic notation and saw a few simple proofs using the definitions. In this section, we will continue using those definitions, but with more examples and some more complicated expressions.

We will also introduce a second proof technique based on limits. Limit proofs are often much shorter and easier than proofs from the definitions, assuming you are comfortable evaluating the relevant limits.

Proofs using the definitions

In this section we provide more examples and exercises that use the definitions to prove bounds.

The first example is annotated with comments about the techniques that are used in many of these proofs. We use the following terminology in our explanation. By lower order term we mean a term that grows slower, and higher order means a term that grows faster. The dominating term is the term that grows the fastest. For instance, in \(x^3+7\, x^2-4\), the \(x^2\) term is a lower order term than \(x^3\), and \(x^3\) is the dominating term. We will discuss common growth rates, including how they relate to each other, in Common Growth Rates.

But for now we assume you know that \(x^5\) grows faster than \(x^3\), for instance.

Example 1

Find a tight bound on \(f(n)=n^8+7n^7-10n^5-2n^4+3n^2-17\).

Solution

We will prove that \(f(n)=\Theta(n^8)\). First, we will prove an upper bound for \(f(n)\). It is clear that when \(n\geq 1\),

\[ \begin{aligned} n^8+7n^7-10n^5-2n^4+3n^2-17 &\leq n^8+7n^7+3n^2 \qquad \text{(1)}\\ &\leq n^8+7n^8+3n^8 \qquad \text{(2)}\\ &=11n^8 \end{aligned} \]

Notes

  1. Negative terms were removed. This gives an upper bound when \(n\geq 0\), because subtracting nonnegative quantities can only make the expression smaller.
  2. Lower-order positive terms were replaced by \(n^8\). Since \(n\geq 1\), we have \(n^7\leq n^8\) and \(n^2\leq n^8\).

Thus, we have \[f(n)=n^8+7n^7-10n^5-2n^4+3n^2-17\leq 11n^8 \mbox{ for all } n\geq 1,\] and we have proved that \(f(n)=O(n^8)\).

Now, we will prove the lower bound for \(f(n)\). When \(n\geq 1\),

\[ \begin{aligned} n^8+7n^7-10n^5-2n^4+3n^2-17 &\geq n^8-10n^5-2n^4-17 \qquad \text{(3)}\\ &\geq n^8-10n^7-2n^7-17n^7 \quad \text{(4)}\\ &=n^8-29n^7 \end{aligned} \]

Notes

  1. Positive lower-order terms were removed. This gives a lower bound because removing positive terms can only make the expression smaller.
  2. Negative lower-order terms were replaced by larger-magnitude negative \(n^7\) terms. Since \(n\geq 1\), we have \(n^5\leq n^7\), \(n^4\leq n^7\), and \(1\leq n^7\), so subtracting those \(n^7\) terms gives a valid lower bound.

Next, we need to find a value \(c>0\) such that \(n^8-29n^7\geq c n^8\). Doing a little algebra, we see that this is equivalent to \((1-c)n^8 \geq 29n^7\). When \(n\geq 1\), we can divide by \(n^7\) and obtain \((1-c)n\geq 29\). Solving for \(c\) we obtain \[c\leq 1-\frac{29}{ n}.\] If \(n\geq 58\), then \(c=1/2\) suffices. We have just shown that if \(n\geq 58\), then \[f(n)=n^8+7n^7-10n^5-2n^4+3n^2-17\geq \frac{1}{2}n^8.\] Thus, \(f(n)=\Omega(n^8)\). Since we have shown that \(f(n)=\Omega(n^8)\) and that \(f(n)=O(n^8)\), we have shown that \(f(n)=\Theta(n^8)\).

Let's see another example of a \(\Omega\) proof. You should note the similarities between this and the second half of the proof in the previous example.

Example 2

Show that \((n \log n - 2\,n + 13)=\Omega(n \log n)\).

Proof

We need to show that there exist positive constants \(c\) and \(n_0\) such that \[c\,n\log n \leq n\log n -2\,n + 13\mbox{ for all } n\geq n_0.\] Since \(n\log n -2\,n \leq n\log n -2\,n+13,\) we will instead show that \[c\,n\log n \leq n\log n -2\,n,\] which is equivalent to \[c\leq 1-\frac{2}{\log n}, \mbox{ when } n > 1.\] If \(n\geq 8\), then \(2/(\log n)\leq 2/3\), and picking \(c=1/3\) suffices. In other words, we have just shown that if \(n\geq 8\), \[\frac{1}{3}\,n\log n \leq n\log n -2\,n.\] Thus if \(c=1/3\) and \(n_0=8\), then for all \(n\geq n_0\), we have \[c\,n\log n \leq n\log n -2\,n \leq n\log n -2\,n + 13.\] Thus \((n \log n - 2\,n + 13)=\Omega(n \log n)\).

Question 1

In the previous proof, we claimed that if \(n\geq 10\), \[\frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{n} \geq \frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{10}.\] Why is this true?

There are a few ways to think about this. First, the larger \(n\) is, the smaller \(\frac{3}{n}\) is, so a smaller amount is being subtracted. But that's perhaps too fuzzy. Let's look at it this way: \[n\geq 10\Rightarrow \frac{10}{3} \leq \frac{n}{3} \Rightarrow \frac{3}{n}\leq \frac{3}{10} \Rightarrow -\frac{3}{n}\geq -\frac{3}{10} \Rightarrow \frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{n}\geq \frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{10}. \]

Example 3

Show that \((\sqrt{2})^{\log n}=O(\sqrt{n})\), where the base of the \(\log\) is 2.

Proof

Assuming you remember your log rules, it is not too hard to see that \[(\sqrt{2})^{\log n}=n^{\log \sqrt{2}}=n^{\log 2^{1/2}}= n^{\frac{1}{2}\log 2}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{n}.\] Thus it is clear that \((\sqrt{2})^{\log n}=O(\sqrt{n})\).

Remark

You may be confused by the previous proof. It seems that we never showed that \((\sqrt{2})^{\log n}\leq c\sqrt{n}\) for some constant \(c\). But we essentially did by showing that \((\sqrt{2})^{\log n}=\sqrt{n}\) since this implies that \((\sqrt{2})^{\log n}\leq 1\sqrt{n}\).

We actually proved something stronger than was required. That is, since we proved the two functions are equal, it is in fact true that \((\sqrt{2})^{\log n}=\Theta(\sqrt{n})\). But we were only asked to prove that \((\sqrt{2})^{\log n}=O(\sqrt{n})\).

In general, if you need to prove a Big-O bound, you may instead prove a \(\Theta\) bound, and the Big-O bound essentially comes along for the ride.

Example 4

Show that \(\log (n!) = O(n\log n)\).

Proof

It should be clear that if \(n\geq 1\), \(n!\leq n^n\) (especially after completing the previous exercise). Taking \(\log\)s of both sides of that inequality, we obtain \[\log (n!)\leq \log (n^n) = n\log n.\] Therefore \(\log (n!) = O(n\log n)\).

The last step used the fact that \(\log (f(n)^a)=a\log (f(n))\), a fact that we assume you have seen previously (but may have forgotten).

Example 5

Prove that if \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) and \(g(n)=O(f(n))\), then \(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\).

Proof

If \(f(n)=O(g(n))\), then there are positive constants \(c_2\) and \(n_{0}^{\prime}\) such that \[f(n)\leq c_2\,g(n)\mbox{ for all }n \ge n_{0}^{\prime}\] Similarly, if \(g(x)=O(f(x))\), then there are positive constants \(c_1^{\prime}\) and \(n_{0}^{\prime\prime}\) such that \[g(n)\leq c_1^{\prime}\,f(n)\mbox{ for all }n \ge n_{0}^{\prime\prime}.\] We can divide this by \(c_1^{\prime}\) to obtain \[\frac{1}{c_1^{\prime}}g(n)\leq f(n)\mbox{ for all }n \ge n_{0}^{\prime\prime}.\] Setting \(c_1=1/c_1^{\prime}\) and \(n_0=\max\{n_0^{\prime},n_0^{\prime\prime}\}\), we have \[c_1 g(n)\leq f(n)\leq c_2\,g(n)\mbox{ for all }n \ge n_{0}.\] Thus, \(f(x)=\Theta(g(x)).\)

Exercise 2

Let \(f(x)=O(g(x))\) and \(g(x)=O(h(x))\). Show that \(f(x)=O(h(x))\). That is, prove Theorem 1 from Asymptotic Properties for Big-O notation.

If \(f(x)=O(g(x))\), then there are positive constants \(c_1\) and \(n_{0}^{\prime}\) such that \[0\leq f(n)\leq c_1\,g(n)\mbox{ for all }n \ge n_{0}^{\prime},\] and if \(g(x)=O(h(x))\), then there are positive constants \(c_2\) and \(n_{0}^{\prime\prime}\) such that \[0\leq g(n)\leq c_2\,h(n)\mbox{ for all }n \ge n_{0}^{\prime\prime}.\] Set \(n_0=\max(n_0^{\prime},n_0^{\prime\prime})\) and \(c_3=c_1\,c_2\). Then \[0\leq f(n)\leq c_1\,g(n)\leq c_1\,c_2\,h(n)=c_3\,h(n) \mbox{ for all }n \ge n_{0}.\] Thus \(f(x)=O(h(x))\).

Proofs using limits

So far we have used the definitions of the various notations in all of our proofs. The following theorem provides another technique that is often much easier, assuming you understand and are comfortable with limits. Make sure you review the concepts on the Mathematical Background page as needed.

Theorem 1: Asymptotic Limit Theorem

Let \(f(n)\) and \(g(n)\) be functions such that \[\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = A.\] Then

  1. If \(A=0\), then \(f(n)=O(g(n))\), and \(f(n)\neq \Theta(g(n))\). That is, \(f(n)=o(g(n))\).
  2. If \(A=\infty\), then \(f(n)=\Omega(g(n))\), and \(f(n)\neq \Theta(g(n))\). That is, \(f(n)=\omega(g(n))\).
  3. If \(A\neq 0\) is finite, then \(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\).

If the above limit does not exist, then you need to resort to using the definitions or using some other technique. Luckily, in the analysis of algorithms the above approach works most of the time.

Theorem 1 does not have an official name, but we will refer to it as the Limit Theorem. Although that name is a bit vague, in our context this is the main theorem related to limits that we are interested in, so it should not cause confusion.

Before we see some examples, let's review a few limits you should know.

Theorem 2

Let \(a\) and \(c\) be real numbers. Then

  1. \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} a = a\)
  2. If \(a>0\), \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} n^a = \infty\)
  3. If \(a<0\), \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} n^a = 0\)
  4. If \(a>1\), \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} a^n = \infty\)
  5. If \(0<a<1\), \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} a^n = 0\)
  6. If \(c>0\), \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} \log_c n = \infty\).

Example 6

The following are examples based on Theorem 2.

  1. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} 13 = 13\)

  2. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} n = \infty\)

  3. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} n^4 = \infty\)

  4. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} n^{1/2} = \infty\)

  5. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} n^{-2} = 0\)

  6. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n} = 0\)

  7. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} 2^{n} = \infty\)

  8. \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \log_2 n = \infty\)

Here are a few more useful properties of limits. Read carefully. These do not apply in all situations.

Theorem 4

Let \(a\) be a finite real number and let \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} f(n)=A\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} g(n)=B\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are finite real numbers. Then

  1. \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} a\,f(n)=a\,A\)

  2. \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} f(n)\pm g(n)=A\pm B\)

  3. \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} f(n)g(n)=AB\)

  4. If \(B\neq 0\), \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=\frac{A}{B}\)

We usually use the results from the previous theorem without explicitly mentioning them.

Example 9

Find a tight bound on \(f(x)=x^8+7x^7-10x^5-2x^4+3x^2-17\) using the Limit Theorem.

Solution: We guess that \(f(x)=\Theta(x^8)\). To prove this, notice that

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{x^8{+}7x^7{-}10x^5{-}2x^4{+}3x^2{-}17}{ x^8} &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{x^8}{ x^8}+ \frac{7x^7}{ x^8}- \frac{10x^5}{ x^8}- \frac{2x^4}{ x^8}+ \frac{3x^2}{ x^8}- \frac{17}{ x^8}\\ &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} 1+ \frac{7}{ x}- \frac{10}{ x^3}- \frac{2}{ x^4}+\frac {3}{ x^6}- \frac{17}{ x^8}\\ &=1+ 0 - 0 - 0 + 0 - 0=1 \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \(f(x)=\Theta(x^8)\) by the Limit Theorem.

Compare the proof above with the proof given in Example 1. It should be pretty obvious that using the Limit Theorem makes the proof a lot easier. Let's see another example that lets us compare the two proof methods.

Example 10

Prove that \(f(x)=x^4-23 x^3 + 12x^2 + 15 x - 21=\Theta(x^4)\).

Proof 1

We will use the definition of \(\Theta\). It is clear that when \(x\geq 1\), \[x^4-23 x^3 + 12x^2 + 15 x - 21\leq x^4+12 x^2+15 x \leq x^4+12 x^4+15 x^4= 28 x^4.\] Also, if \(x\geq 88\), then \(\frac{1}{2}x^4\geq 44x^3\) or \(-44x^3\geq -\frac{1}{2}x^4\), so we have that \[x^4-23 x^3 + 12x^2 + 15 x - 21\geq x^4-23 x^3 - 21\geq x^4 - 23 x^3 - 21 x^3= x^4 - 44 x^3\geq \frac{1}{2}x^4.\]

Thus \[\frac{1}{2}x^4\leq x^4-23 x^3 + 12x^2 + 15 x - 21\leq 28 x^4, \mbox{ for all } x\geq 88.\] We have shown that \(f(x)=x^4-23 x^3 + 12x^2 + 15 x - 21=\Theta(x^4).\) \(\square\)

If you did not follow the steps in this first proof, you should really review your algebra rules.

Proof 2

Since

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{ x^4-23 x^3 + 12x^2 + 15 x - 21}{x^4} &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{x^4}{ x^4} -\frac{23x^3}{ x^4} + \frac{12x^2}{ x^4} + \frac{15x}{ x^4} - \frac{21}{ x^4}\\ &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} 1 - \frac{23}{ x}+ \frac{12}{ x^2} + \frac{15}{ x^3} - \frac{21}{ x^4}\\ &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} 1 - 0 + 0 + 0 - 0=1, \end{aligned} \]

\(f(x)=x^4-23 x^3 + 12x^2 + 15 x - 21=\Theta(x^4)\) \(\square\)

It should be pretty clear from Example 10 that you should use the Limit Theorem instead of the definition whenever possible!

Example 11

Prove that \(n(n+1)/2=O(n^3)\) using the Limit Theorem.

Proof

Compare \(n(n+1)/2\) with \(n^3\): \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n(n+1)/2}{n^3} &= \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n^2+n}{2n^3} \\ &= \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{2n^2}\right) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \] By case 1 of the Limit Theorem, \(n(n+1)/2=o(n^3)\), and therefore \(n(n+1)/2=O(n^3)\).

Now is probably a good time to recall a very useful theorem for computing limits, called l'Hopital's Rule. The version presented here is restricted to limits where the variable approaches infinity since those are the only limits of interest in our context.

Theorem 5: l'Hopital's Rule

Let \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) be differentiable functions. If \[\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}g(x)=0 \mbox{ or}\] \[\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}g(x)=\infty,\] then \[ \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{f(x)}{ g(x)}= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{ g^{\prime}(x)}\]

Example 12

Since \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} 3x=\infty\) and \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} x^2=\infty\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{3x}{x^2} &=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{3}{2x} \, \, \, \, \mbox{(l'Hopital)}\\ &=\frac{3}{2}\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{1}{x}\\ &=\frac{3}{2}\,0\\ &=0. \end{aligned} \]

Example 13

Since \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} 3x^2+4x-9=\infty\) and \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} 12x=\infty\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{3x^2+4x-9}{12x} &=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{6x+4}{12} \, \, \, \, \mbox{(l'Hopital)}\\ &=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{1}{2}x+\frac{1}{3}\\ &=\infty \end{aligned} \]

Now let's apply it to proving asymptotic bounds.

Example 14

Show that \(\log x=O(x)\).

Proof

We want to compare \(\log x\) with \(x\), so we consider the ratio \[ \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{\log x}{x}. \] At this point, the limit is not obvious from the basic limit rules: both \(\log x\) and \(x\) grow without bound. This is exactly the kind of situation where l'Hopital's Rule can help.

Before applying l'Hopital's Rule, we must check that its preconditions are met. Since \[ \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\log x=\infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}x=\infty, \] the ratio has the form \(\infty/\infty\), so l'Hopital's Rule applies.

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{\log x}{x} &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{1/x}{1} \qquad \text{(l'Hopital)} \\ &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{1}{x} \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Since the limit is 0, \(\log x=O(x)\) by case 1 of the Limit Theorem.

This example illustrates an important habit: always verify the preconditions of l'Hopital's Rule before applying it. The rule only applies to ratios with the form \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\).

Example 15

Prove that \(x^3=O(2^x)\).

Proof

We want to compare \(x^3\) with \(2^x\), so we consider the ratio \[ \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{x^3}{2^x}. \] Both \(x^3\) and \(2^x\) go to infinity as \(x\rightarrow\infty\), so the ratio has the form \(\infty/\infty\). Therefore, l'Hopital's Rule applies.

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{x^3}{2^x} &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{3x^2}{2^x\ln 2} \qquad \text{(l'Hopital)}. \end{aligned} \]

This new ratio is still of the form \(\infty/\infty\), so we can apply l'Hopital's Rule again:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{3x^2}{2^x\ln 2} &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{6x}{2^x(\ln 2)^2} \qquad \text{(l'Hopital)}. \end{aligned} \]

The ratio is still of the form \(\infty/\infty\), so we apply l'Hopital's Rule one more time:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{6x}{2^x(\ln 2)^2} &= \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{6}{2^x(\ln 2)^3} \qquad \text{(l'Hopital)}. \end{aligned} \]

Now the numerator is the constant \(6\), while the denominator goes to infinity. Therefore, \[ \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{6}{2^x(\ln 2)^3}=0. \] Thus, \[ \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{x^3}{2^x}=0. \] By case 1 of the Limit Theorem, \(x^3=o(2^x)\), and therefore \(x^3=O(2^x)\).

Evaluate 1

Students were asked to prove that \(7^x\) is an upper bound for \(5^x\), but that it is not a tight bound. Evaluate their attempts. If they are incorrect, explain where they went wrong.

  1. This is true if and only if \(7^x\) always grows faster than \(5^x\) which means \(7^x-5^x>0\) for all \(x\neq 0\). If it is a tight bound, then \(7^x-5^x=0\), which is only true for \(x=0\). So \(7^x\) is an upper bound on \(5^x\), but not a tight bound.

  2. \({\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{5^x}{7^x} = \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{x\log 5}{x\log 7}.}\) Both go to infinity, but \(x\log 7\) gets there faster, showing that \(5^x=O(7^x)\).

  3. \({\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{7^x}{5^x} = \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\left(\frac{7}{5}\right)^x=\infty}\) since \(7/5>1\). Thus \(5^x=O(7^x)\) by the limit theorem.

  1. \(7^x\) grows faster than \(5^x\) does not mean \(7^x-5^x>0\) for all \(x\neq 0\). For one thing, we are really only concerned about positive values of \(x\). Further, we are specifically concerned about very large values of \(x\). In other words, we want something to be true for all \(x\) that are 'large enough'. Also, this statement does not take into account constant factors. Similarly, a tight bound does not imply that \(7^x-5^x=0\).

    The bottom line is that this response does not show a clear understanding of what an asymptotic upper bound means, and it does not provide a valid proof.

  2. This one has several problems. First, the application of l'Hopital's rule is incorrect. The result should be \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{5^x\log 5}{7^x\log 7}\), which should make it obvious that l'Hopital's rule doesn't actually help in this case. (The key to this one is to do a little algebra.) The next problem is the statement 'but \(x\log 7\) gets there faster'. What exactly does that mean? Asymptotically faster, or just faster? If the former, it needs to be proven. If the latter, that isn't enough to prove relative growth rates. Finally, even if this showed that \(5^x=O(7^x)\), that only shows that \(7^x\) is an upper bound on \(5^x\). It does not show that the bound is not tight. The bottom line is that bad algebra combined with vague statements falls way short of a correct proof.

  3. This proof is very close to being correct. The main problem is that they only stated that \(5^x=O(7^x)\), but they also needed to show that \(5^x\neq\Theta(7^x)\). It turns out that the theorem they mention also gives them that. So all they needed to add is 'and \(5^x\neq \Theta(7^x)\)' at the end. Technically, there is another problem—they should have taken the limit of \(5^x/7^x\). What they really showed using the limit theorem is that \(7^x=\omega(5^x)\), which is equivalent to \(5^x=o(7^x)\). It isn't a major problem, but technically the limit theorem does not directly give them the result they say it does. If you are trying to prove that \(f(x)\) is bounded by \(g(x)\), put \(f(x)\) on the top and \(g(x)\) on the bottom.

Remember to always verify that l'Hopital's Rule applies before just blindly taking derivatives. You can actually get the incorrect answer if you apply it when it should not be applied.

Example 16

Find and prove a simple tight bound for \(\sqrt{5n^2-4n+12}\).

Solution

We will show that \(\sqrt{5n^2-4n+12}=\Theta(n)\). Since we are letting \(n\) go to infinity, we can assume that \(n>0\). In this case, \(n=\sqrt{n^2}\). Using this, we can see that \[\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sqrt{5n^2-4n+12}}{n} =\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{5n^2-4n+12}{n^2}} =\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{5 - \frac{4}{n}+\frac{12}{n^2}} =\sqrt{5}. \] Therefore, by the third case of the Limit Theorem, \(\sqrt{5n^2-4n+12}=\Theta(n)\).

Example 17

Find and prove a simple tight bound for \(n\log(n^2)+(n-1)^2\log(n/2)\).

Solution

First notice that \[ n\log(n^2)+(n-1)^2\log(n/2) = 2n\log n + (n-1)^2(\log n - \log 2). \] We claim that this expression is \(\Theta(n^2\log n)\). To use the Limit Theorem, compare it with \(n^2\log n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{n\log(n^2)+(n-1)^2\log(n/2)}{n^2\log n} &= \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{2n\log n + (n-1)^2(\log n - \log 2)}{n^2\log n} \\ &= \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \left( \frac{2}{n} + \frac{(n-1)^2}{n^2} \cdot \frac{\log n - \log 2}{\log n} \right) \\ &= \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \left( \frac{2}{n} + \left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^2 \left(1-\frac{\log 2}{\log n}\right) \right) \\ &= 0+(1-0)^2(1-0) \\ &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

Since the limit is a positive finite constant, the Limit Theorem implies that \[ n\log(n^2)+(n-1)^2\log(n/2)=\Theta(n^2\log n). \]

Exercise 8

Find and prove a simple tight bound for \((n^2-1)^5\). You may use either the formal definition of \(\Theta\) or the Limit Theorem. (The solution uses the Limit Theorem.)

We claim that \((n^2-1)^5=\Theta(n^{10})\). To use the Limit Theorem, compare \((n^2-1)^5\) with \(n^{10}\): \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(n^2-1)^5}{n^{10}} &=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{n^2-1}{n^2}\right)^5 \\ &=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{n^2}\right)^5 \\ &=1. \end{aligned} \] Since the limit is a positive finite constant, the Limit Theorem implies that \((n^2-1)^5=\Theta(n^{10})\).

Exercise 9

Find and prove a simple tight bound for \(2^{n+1}+5^{n-1}\). You may use either the formal definition of \(\Theta\) or the Limit Theorem. (The solution uses the Limit Theorem.)

We claim that \(2^{n+1}+5^{n-1}=\Theta(5^n)\). To use the Limit Theorem, compare \(2^{n+1}+5^{n-1}\) with \(5^n\): \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2^{n+1}+5^{n-1}}{5^n} &=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{2^{n+1}}{5^n}+\frac{5^{n-1}}{5^n}\right) \\ &=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(2\left(\frac{2}{5}\right)^n+\frac{1}{5}\right) \\ &=0+\frac{1}{5} \\ &=\frac{1}{5}. \end{aligned} \] Since the limit is a positive finite constant, the Limit Theorem implies that \(2^{n+1}+5^{n-1}=\Theta(5^n)\).

We could also use \(\Theta(5^{n-1})\), but \(\Theta(5^n)\) is simpler and more standard.
