Common Growth Rates

In this section we compare the relative growth rates of several functions that appear frequently in algorithm analysis. These comparisons help us decide which terms dominate as the input size \(n\) becomes large.

The table below gives values for several common functions over a wide range of input sizes. The rows double \(n\) each time, which makes it easier to see long-term behavior than a table that only uses consecutive small values. Notice, for example, how slowly \(\log_2 n\) grows, how \(\sqrt n\) stays below \(n\), and how quickly \(2^n\) becomes enormous.

Expected Observations

  1. Constant factors versus growth rate: At the beginning, \(100n\) is much larger than the other two functions. For example, at \(n=20\), the values are \[ n^2=400,\qquad 10n=200,\qquad 100n=2000. \] The first important change occurs at \(n=10\), where \[ n^2=10n=100. \] After that, \(n^2\) is larger than \(10n\). The second important change occurs at \(n=100\), where \[ n^2=100n=10000. \] After \(n=100\), \(n^2\) is larger than both linear functions. This shows that a large constant factor can dominate for small inputs, but a function from a faster growth class eventually overtakes it.
  2. Large constants versus growth class: At first, \(10000n\) is much larger than the others. For example, at \(n=20\), \[ 10000n=200000,\qquad 100n^2=40000,\qquad n^3=8000. \] All three functions meet at \(n=100\), since \[ 10000n=100n^2=n^3=1000000. \] Before \(n=100\), the linear function with the large constant is largest. After \(n=100\), the cubic function becomes largest, followed by the quadratic function, then the linear function. This illustrates the long-term ordering \[ n \ll n^2 \ll n^3, \] even when the lower-growth functions have large constant factors.

  3. Lower-order terms: At first, \(n^3+12345\) is the largest function because the added constant is large compared with \(n^3\) and \(100n^2\). The first important change occurs between \(n=11\) and \(n=12\), because \[ 100n^2 > 12345 \] starting at \(n=12\). After that, \(n^3+100n^2\) is the largest of the three functions.

    A second important change happens when \(n\) is between about 40 and 100 where \(n^3+12345\) and \(n^3\) start looking more and more alike. This happens because the relative difference between them, 12345, is small in comparison to how fast the difference between these functions and \(n^3+100n^2\) is growing.

    Finally, as \(n\) gets very large, the functions start to look almost the same. This is because as \(n\) gets larger, the lower order terms \(100n^2\) and \(12345\) are very small in comparison with \(n^3\), so all three functions are relatively close in value to each othr. By very large \(n\), all three functions have the same long-term growth rate, \(\Theta(n^3)\), even though they can look different for smaller values.

  4. Do not judge too quickly: On the initial range \(1\le n\le 10\), the exponential function does not look impressive. At \(n=10\), \[ 2^n=1024,\qquad 300n^3=300000,\qquad 5000n^2=500000. \] So the graph may suggest that \(2^n\) is the smallest (by far!), that \(300n^3\) is faster, and \(5000n^2\) is the fastest. As Maximum n increases, \(300n^3\) catches up and passes \(5000n^2\) between \(n=16\) and \(n=17\), which makes sense because a cubic function should grow faster than a quadratic. It doesn't take long after that for \(2^n\) to catch up and pass both of the other functions between \(n=21\) and \(n=22\). If we further increase \(n\) to 30 or 40, the other two funcions are so much smaller they barely register on the graph. We will soon show that that this also makes perfect sense when we prove that any exponential function with a base greater than 1 grows faster than any polynomial.

    The lesson to be learned here is to not use graphs, especially for small values of \(n\), to draw conclusions about growth rates.

  5. Logarithmic growth: On \(1\le n\le 100\), \(\log_2 n\) is already much smaller than the other functions. At \(n=100\), \[ \log_2 n\approx 6.64,\qquad \sqrt n=10,\qquad n=100,\qquad n\log_2 n\approx 664. \] When Maximum n is increased to \(10000\), the separation is much clearer: \[ \log_2 n\approx 13.29,\qquad \sqrt n=100,\qquad n=10000,\qquad n\log_2 n\approx 132877. \] The logarithm remains very small, \(\sqrt n\) grows more slowly than \(n\), and \(n\log n\) pulls away from \(n\). This supports the following ordering that we will prove later in this section. \[ \log n \ll \sqrt n \ll n \ll n\log n. \]

    This also illustartes a sometimes misunderstood point: Notice that even though \(\log n\) is a slow growing function, it still grows. Thus, when we compare \(n\) to \(n \log n\), the latter grows faster because it is multiplied by a function that gets larger as \(n\) increases. It doesn't matter how slowly it grows—some growth is faster than no growth.

  6. Same class, different appearance: One surprising observation at the beginning is that \(n^2+1000n\) is much larger than \(5n^2\), even though both, and the 5 in front of the \(n^2\) would make us think it should be larger. Notice that at \(n=100\), that is not the case: \[ n^2=10000,\quad n^2+n=10100,\quad n^2+1000n=110000,\quad 5n^2=50000. \] The important crossover occurs at \(n=250\), because \(n^2+1000n=5n^2\) at that point. Before \(n=250\), \(n^2+1000n\) is larger than \(5n^2\). After \(n=250\), \(5n^2\) is larger. Now it is starting to match our intuition!

    When Maximum n is increased to \(10000\), the lower-order terms \(1000n\) and \(n\) matter much less compared with \(n^2\). Thus, \(n^2\), \(n^2+n\), and \(n^2+1000n\) start resembling each other, and \(5n^5\) is somewhere under 5 times as large. Since all four functions are \(\Theta(n^2)\), they will remain in these relative positions as \(n\) goes to infinity, with the first three all being very close to the same value, and the fourth one approaching about 5 times the value of the others.

  7. Exponential bases: On the initial range \(1\le n\le 10\), all four functions are still fairly small, but the larger bases are already pulling ahead. At \(n=10\), \[ 1.001^n\approx 1.01,\qquad 1.1^n\approx 2.59,\qquad 1.5^n\approx 57.67,\qquad 2^n=1024. \] By \(n=25\), the separation is much larger: \[ 1.001^n\approx 1.03,\qquad 1.1^n\approx 10.83,\qquad 1.5^n\approx 25251,\qquad 2^n=33554432. \] When \(2^n\) is unchecked, the vertical scale adjusts and the differences among the remaining functions become easier to see. When \(1.5^n\) is also unchecked, the much slower growth of \(1.001^n\) and \(1.1^n\) becomes visible. This is because each larger exponential grows so much faster than the next one that the others seem almost constant-valued in comparison. As we will show later, if \(1\lt a\lt b\), then \(a^n=o(b^n)\). In other words, larger base of an exponention means faster growth rate.
  8. Logarithm bases: The three logarithms keep the same relative order: \[ \log_2 n > \ln n > \log_{10} n. \] For example, at \(n=100\), \[ \log_2 n\approx 6.64,\qquad \ln n\approx 4.61,\qquad \log_{10} n=2. \] At \(n=10000\), \[ \log_2 n\approx 13.29,\qquad \ln n\approx 9.21,\qquad \log_{10} n=4. \] The values increase, but the ratios stay the same. For example, \[ \frac{\log_2 n}{\ln n}\approx 1.44,\qquad \frac{\ln n}{\log_{10} n}\approx 2.30,\qquad \frac{\log_2 n}{\log_{10} n}\approx 3.32 \] at both \(n=100\) and \(n=10000\). Their positions do not cross because changing the base of a logarithm only multiplies by a constant. Thus, \[ \log_2 n=\Theta(\ln n)=\Theta(\log_{10} n). \]

    Later we will prove that logarithms with different bases all have the same growth rate.

  9. Powers of logarithms: On the initial range \(1\le n\le 10\), the order may not match students' intuition. At \(n=10\), \[ \log_2 n\approx 3.32,\qquad (\log_2 n)^2\approx 11.04,\qquad (\log_2 n)^3\approx 36.65,\qquad n=10. \] So \((\log_2 n)^3\) is larger than \(n\) at \(n=10\). By \(n=100\), \[ \log_2 n\approx 6.64,\qquad (\log_2 n)^2\approx 44.14,\qquad (\log_2 n)^3\approx 293.55,\qquad n=100, \] so \((\log_2 n)^3\) is still larger than \(n\). But by \(n=10000\), \[ \log_2 n\approx 13.29,\qquad (\log_2 n)^2\approx 176.56,\qquad (\log_2 n)^3\approx 2346.64,\qquad n=10000. \] Now \(n\) is larger. The crossover between \(n\) and \((\log_2 n)^3\) occurs between \(n=981\) and \(n=982\). This is a good example of why slow-growing functions can still look large over small or moderate ranges. In the long run, any fixed power of a logarithm grows slower than any positive power of \(n\).

The explorer should illustrate several important lessons. Constant factors can make one function much larger than another for small inputs, but they do not change the long-term growth class. Lower-order terms can also matter for small inputs, but they become less important as \(n\) grows. Most importantly, a graph can be misleading if the viewing window is too small. A function that looks larger on \(1 \le n \le 20\) may be overtaken later by a function with a faster growth rate. Graphs are useful for building intuition, but asymptotic relationships should be justified using definitions, limits, and theorems, not by visual inspection alone.

Common Growth Rate Facts

Next we present some of the most important results about the relative growth rate of some common functions. We will ask you to prove each of them. The Limit Theorem and l'Hopital's rule will help you do so. You will notice that most of the theorems are using little-o, not Big-O. Hopefully you remember the difference. If not, review those definitions before continuing.

We begin with something that is pretty intuitive: higher powers grow faster than lower powers.

Theorem 1

Let \(a<b\) be real numbers. Then \(n^a=o(n^b)\).

Example 1

According to Theorem 1, \(n^2=o(n^3)\) and \(n^5=o(n^{5.1})\).

Exercise 2

Prove Theorem 1. (Hint: Use the Limit Theorem and do a little algebra before you try to compute the limit.)

Since \(a<b\), \(b-a>0\). Therefore, \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{n^a}{n^b} = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} n^{a-b} = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{1}{n^{b-a}}=0\). By the Limit Theorem, \(n^a=o(n^b)\).

The next theorem tells us that exponentials with different bases do not grow at the same rate. More specifically, the higher the base, the faster the growth rate.

Theorem 2

Let \(0<a<b\) be real numbers. Then \(a^n=o(b^n)\).

Example 2

According to Theorem 2, \(2^n=o(5^n)\) and \(4^n=o(4.5^n)\).

Exercise 3

Prove Theorem 2.

Since \(a<b\), \(a/b<1\). Therefore, \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{a^n}{b^n} = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = 0\). By the Limit Theorem, \(a^n=o(b^n)\).

Recall that a logarithmic function is the inverse of an exponential function. That is, \(b^{x} = n\) is equivalent to \(x = \log_{b} n\). The following identity is very useful.

Theorem 3

Let \(a\), \(b\), and \(x\) be positive real numbers with \(a\neq 1\) and \(b\neq 1\). Then \[\log_a x = \frac{\log_b x}{\log_b a}.\]

Example 3

Most calculators can compute \(\ln n\) or \(\log_{10} n\), but are unable to compute logarithms with any given base. But Theorem 3 allows you to do so. For instance, you can compute \(\log_2 39\) as \(\log_{10} 39/\log_{10} 2\).

Notice that the formula in Theorem 3 can be rearranged as \((\log_b a)(\log_a x) = \log_b x.\) This form should make it evident that changing the base of a logarithm just changes the value by a constant amount. This leads to the following result.

Corollary 1

Let \(a\) and \(b\) be positive real numbers with \(a\neq 1\) and \(b\neq 1\).

Then \(\log_a n=\Theta(\log_b n)\).

Proof

Follows from the definition of \(\Theta\) and Theorem 3.

Example 4

According to Corollary 1, \(\log_2 n =\Theta(\log_{10}n)\) and \(\ln n =\Theta(\log_2 n)\).

Corollary 1 is stating that all logarithms have the same rate of growth regardless of their bases. That is, the base of a logarithm does not matter when it is used in asymptotic notation. Because of this, the base is often omitted in asymptotic notation. In computer science, it is usually safe to assume that the base of logarithms is \(2\) if it is not specified.

Exercise 4

Indicate whether each of the following is true (T) or false (F).

  1. \(2^n=\Theta(3^n)\)

  2. \(2^n=o(3^n)\)

  3. \(3^n=O(2^n)\)

  4. \(\log_3 n=\Theta(\log_2 n)\)

  5. \(\log_2 n=O(\log_3 n)\)

  6. \(\log_{10} n=o(\log_3 n)\)

  1. False. \(3^n\) grows faster than \(2^n\), so they do not have the same growth rate.
  2. True. \(2^n\) grows slower than \(3^n\), so \(2^n=o(3^n)\).
  3. False. \(3^n\) grows faster than \(2^n\), so it is not \(O(2^n)\).
  4. True. Logarithms with different bases have the same growth rate, so \(\log_3 n=\Theta(\log_2 n)\).
  5. True. Since \(\log_2 n=\Theta(\log_3 n)\), it is also true that \(\log_2 n=O(\log_3 n)\).
  6. False. \(\log_{10} n\) and \(\log_3 n\) have the same growth rate, so \(\log_{10} n\) is not \(o(\log_3 n)\).

Next we see that logarithms grow slower than positive powers of \(n\).

Theorem 4

Let \(b>0\) and \(c>1\) be real numbers. Then \(\log_c(n)=o(n^b)\).

Example 5

According to Theorem 4, \(\log_2 n= o(n^2)\), \(\log_{10} n= o(n^{1.01})\), and \(\ln n =o(\sqrt{n})\).

Exercise 5

Prove Theorem 4. (Hint: This is easy if you use the Limit Theorem and l'Hopital's rule.)

Using l'Hopital's rule, we have \(\displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{\log_c(n)}{n^b} = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{n\ln(c)}}{b\, n^{b-1}} = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\ln(c)b\, n^b} =0\) since \(b>0\). Thus, the Limit Theorem tells us that \(\log_c n=o(n^b)\).

More generally, the next theorem states that any positive power of a logarithm grows slower than any positive power of \(n\). Since this one is a little tricky, we will provide the proof. In case you have not seen this notation before, you should know that \(\log^a n\) means \((\log n)^a\), which is not the same thing as \(\log (n^a)\).

Theorem 5

Let \(a>0\), \(b>0\), and \(c>0\) with \(c\neq 1\). Then \(\log_c^a(n)=o(n^b)\). In other words, any positive power of a logarithm grows slower than any positive power of \(n\).

Proof

First, we need to know that if \(r>0\) is a constant and \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} f(n)=C\), then \[ \displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(f(n))^r = \left(\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)\right)^r = C^r. \] Using this and the limit computed in the proof of Theorem 4, we have \[ \displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{\log_c^a(n)}{n^b} = \displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \left(\frac{\log_c(n)}{n^{b/a}}\right)^a = \left( \displaystyle \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{\log_c(n)}{n^{b/a}} \right)^a = 0^a = 0. \] Thus, the Limit Theorem tells us that \(\log_c^a(n)=o(n^b)\).

Example 6

According to Theorem 5, \(\log_2^4 n= o(n^2)\), \(\ln^{10} n =o(\sqrt{n})\), and

\(\log_{10}^{1,000,000} n= o(n^{.00000001})\).

Finally, any exponential function with base larger than \(1\) grows faster than any polynomial.

Theorem 6

Let \(a>0\) and \(b>1\) be real numbers. Then \(n^a=o(b^n)\).

There are several ways to prove Theorem 6, including repeated applications of l'Hopital's rule, induction, or more algebraic arguments. The main idea is that repeated differentiation reduces the power of \(n\), while an exponential function remains exponential. Since the techniques are beyond what we generally need in the course, we will omit a proof (and, perhaps more importantly, we will not ask you to provide a proof!).

Example 7

According to Theorem 6, it is easy to see that \(n^2=o(2^n)\), \(n^{15}=o(1.5^n)\), and \(n^{1,000,000}=o(1.0000001^n)\).

Growth Rate Hierarchy

An alternative notation for little-o is \(\ll\). In other words, \(f(n)=o(g(n))\) iff \(f(n)\ll g(n)\). This notation is useful in certain contexts, including the following comparison of the growth rate of common functions. The previous theorems in this section provide proofs of some of these relationships. The others are given without proof.

Theorem 7

Here are some relationships between the growth rates of common functions: \[c \ll \log n \ll \log^{2} n \ll \sqrt n \ll n \ll n \log n \ll n^{1.1} \ll n^{2} \ll n^3 \ll n^4 \ll 2^n\ll 3^n\ll n!\ll n^n\]

You should convince yourself that each of the relationships given in the previous theorem is correct. You should also memorize them or (preferably) understand why each one is correct so you can 'recreate' the theorem.

  11. Multiplying by a Slow-Growing Function: We think of \(\log n\) as a slow-growing function. Does that mean that \(f(n)\log n\) grows slower than \(f(n)\)? In general, if we multiply a function by a slow-growing function, does it make the result grow slower? Explain.
  1. Answer: Doubling the input size lets us see long-term growth more clearly. Consecutive small values often show only short-term behavior, where constants and lower-order terms may still have a large effect.
  2. Answer: A graph only shows the functions over the chosen viewing window. A small window can make one function appear larger even if another function eventually overtakes it. Graphs are useful for intuition, but asymptotic comparisons should be justified with definitions, limits, and theorems.
  3. Answer: Multiplying by a positive constant can change the actual values of the function, sometimes by a lot, but it does not change the asymptotic growth class. For example, \(n\), \(10n\), and \(100n\) are all \(\Theta(n)\).
  4. Answer: Lower-order terms grow more slowly than the dominant term. As \(n\) becomes large, the dominant term contributes most of the function's value, so lower-order terms have less relative effect. For example, \(n^3+100n^2+234=\Theta(n^3)\).
  5. Answer: The change-of-base formula shows that changing the base of a logarithm only multiplies the logarithm by a constant. Since constant factors do not affect \(\Theta\) classes, \(\log_2 n\), \(\log_{10} n\), and \(\ln n\) all have the same growth rate.
  6. Answer: Logarithms grow slower than positive powers of \(n\), and positive powers of \(n\) grow slower than exponentials with base greater than \(1\). In simplified form: \[ \log n \ll n^a \ll b^n \] for constants \(a>0\) and \(b>1\).
  7. Answer: In this section, \(f(n)\ll g(n)\) means \(f(n)=o(g(n))\). That is, \(f(n)\) grows strictly slower than \(g(n)\).
  8. Answer: The fastest-growing term usually determines the \(\Theta\) bound. For example, \(n^5+n^3+1900+n^7+21n+n^2=\Theta(n^7)\), because \(n^7\) dominates the other terms.
  9. Answer: The correct ranking is

    \(500n\) \(3n\log n+100n \sim 10n\log n\) \(7n^2+50n+12 \sim n^2\) \(n^3-20n^2 \sim 0.01n^3+900n\) \(2^n+n^4\)

    The constants and lower-order terms can affect the function values for small inputs, but they do not change the dominant growth rate.
  10. Answer: Both functions contain a factor of \(n\). The difference is that \(n\log n\) has an additional factor of \(\log n\), while \(cn\) only has the constant factor \(c\). Since \(\log n\) eventually grows larger than any fixed constant, \(n\log n\) eventually grows faster than \(cn\). Thus \(cn=o(n\log n)\).
  11. Answer: No. Although \(\log n\) grows slowly, it still grows. Multiplying \(f(n)\) by \(\log n\) multiplies \(f(n)\) by a factor that eventually becomes larger and larger. For functions that are eventually positive, \(f(n)\log n\) grows faster than \(f(n)\), not slower.
