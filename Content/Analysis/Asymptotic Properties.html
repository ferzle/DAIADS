Asymptotic Properties and Proofs

Introduction

In this section, we will present some of the properties of Big-O, \(\Theta\), and \(\Omega\) notation. We will also provide proofs for some of the results. The rest can be proven without too much difficulty using the definitions of the notations.

Properties of the Notations

Before we present the properties of asymptotic notation, it helps to recall a few properties of ordinary comparisons. For real numbers \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\), you already know facts such as these:

Property Ordinary comparison
Transitivity If \(x\leq y\) and \(y\leq z\), then \(x\leq z\).
Multiplying by a positive constant If \(x\leq y\) and \(a>0\), then \(a\,x\leq a\,y\).

Asymptotic notation has similar properties. You can think of \(O\) as being somewhat like \(\leq\), \(\Theta\) as being somewhat like \(=\), and \(\Omega\) as being somewhat like \(\geq\). Many familiar properties of \(\leq\), \(=\), and \(\geq\) have analogs for \(O\), \(\Theta\), and \(\Omega\).

The analogy is useful, but it is not exact. For example, constants cannot simply be ignored in ordinary inequalities, but constant factors can be ignored in asymptotic notation. So the familiar comparison symbols are a guide, not a replacement for the definitions.

Theorem 1: Transitivity

The transitive property holds for Big-O, \(\Theta\), and \(\Omega\). That is,

  • If \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) and \(g(n)=O(h(n))\), then \(f(n)=O(h(n))\).
  • If \(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\) and \(g(n)=\Theta(h(n))\), then \(f(n)=\Theta(h(n))\).
  • If \(f(n)=\Omega(g(n))\) and \(g(n)=\Omega(h(n))\), then \(f(n)=\Omega(h(n))\).

Proof

Left as an exercise.

Theorem 1 is pretty intuitive. For instance, when applied to Big-O notation, Theorem 1 is essentially stating that if \(g(n)\) is an upper bound on \(f(n)\) and \(h(n)\) is an upper bound on \(g(n)\), then \(h(n)\) is an upper bound for \(f(n)\). Put another way, if \(f(n)\) grows no faster than \(g(n)\) and \(g(n)\) grows no faster than \(h(n)\), then \(f(n)\) grows no faster than \(h(n)\). This makes perfect sense if you think about it for a few minutes.

Example 1

Let's take it for granted that \(4n^2+3n+17=O(n^3)\) and \(n^3=O(n^4)\) (both of which you should be able to easily prove at this point). According to Theorem 1, we can conclude that \(4n^2+3n+17=O(n^4)\).

Theorem 2: Scaling by a constant factor

If \(f(n)= O(g(n))\), then for any \(k > 0\), \(k f(n) = O(g(n))\). Similarly for \(\Theta\) and \(\Omega\).

Proof

We will give the proof for Big-O notation. The other two proofs are similar. Assume \(f(n)= O(g(n))\). Then by the definition of Big-O, there are positive constants \(c\) and \(n_0\) such that \(f(n)\leq c\,g(n)\) for all \(n\geq n_0\). Thus, if \(n\geq n_0\), \[k\,f(n)\leq k\,c\,g(n)=c^{\prime}g(n),\] where \(c^{\prime}=k\,c\) is a positive constant. By the definition of Big-O, \(k f(n) = O(g(n))\).

Example 2

Example 6 showed that \(\frac{1}{2} n^{2} + 3\,n=\Theta(n^{2})\). We can use Theorem 2 to conclude that \(n^{2} + 6\,n=\Theta(n^{2})\) since \(n^{2} + 6\,n=2\left(\frac{1}{2} n^{2} + 3\,n\right).\)

Perhaps now is a good time to point out a related issue. Typically, we do not include constants inside asymptotic notations. For instance, although it is technically correct to say that \(34n^3+2n^2-45n+5=O(5n^3)\) (or \(O(50n^3)\), or any other constant you care to place there), it is best to just say it is \(O(n^3)\). In particular, \(\Theta(1)\) is often preferable to \(\Theta(k)\).

Theorem 3: Sums

If \(f_{1}(n) = O(g_{1}(n))\) and \(f_{2}(n) = O(g_{2}(n))\), then \[ f_{1}(n)+f_{2}(n) = O(g_{1}(n)+g_{2}(n))=O(\max\{g_{1}(n),g_{2}(n)\}). \] The first part says that the sum of the two functions is bounded above by a constant multiple of \(g_1(n)+g_2(n)\). The second part uses the fact that, because \(g_1\) and \(g_2\) are nonnegative, \[ g_1(n)+g_2(n)\leq 2\max\{g_1(n),g_2(n)\}. \] So using \(g_1(n)+g_2(n)\) or \(\max\{g_1(n),g_2(n)\}\) gives the same Big-O bound up to a constant factor. The statement holds if we replace \(O\) with \(\Theta\) or \(\Omega\) throughout; the proofs are similar.

Proof

We will prove the assertion for Big-O. Assume \(f_{1}(n) = O(g_{1}(n))\) and \(f_{2}(n) = O(g_{2}(n))\). Then there exist positive constants \(c_1\) and \(n_1\) such that for all \(n\geq n_1\), \[f_1(n)\leq c_1g_1(n),\] and there exist positive constants \(c_2\) and \(n_2\) such that for all \(n\geq n_2\), \[f_2(n)\leq c_2g_2(n).\] Let \(c_0=\max\{c_1,c_2\}\) and \(n_0=\max\{n_1,n_2\}\). Since \(n_0\) is at least as large as \(n_1\) and \(n_2\), then for all \(n\geq n_0\), \(f_1(n)\leq c_1g_1(n)\) and \(f_2(n)\leq c_2g_2(n)\). Similarly, if \(f_1(n)\leq c_1g_1(n)\), then clearly \(f_1(n)\leq c_0g_1(n)\) since \(c_0\) is at least as big as \(c_1\) (and similarly for \(f_2\)). Then for all \(n\geq n_0\), we have

\[ \begin{aligned} f_1(n)+f_2(n)&\leq c_1g_1(n)+c_2g_2(n)\\ &\leq c_0g_1(n)+c_0g_2(n)\\ &= c_0[g_1(n)+g_2(n)]\\ &\leq c_0[\max\{g_1(n),g_2(n)\}+\max\{g_1(n),g_2(n)\}]\\ &= 2c_0\max\{g_1(n),g_2(n)\}\\ &= c\max\{g_1(n),g_2(n)\}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(c=2c_0\). By the definition of Big-O, we have shown that \(f_{1}(n)+f_{2}(n)=O(\max\{g_{1}(n),g_{2}(n)\})\).

Notice that in this proof we used \(c=2\max\{c_1,c_2\}\) and \(n_0=\max\{n_1,n_2\}\).

Theorem 3 is the reason we usually keep only the fastest-growing term when simplifying a sum inside asymptotic notation. For example, \(O(n^2+n^3)\) is usually written as \(O(n^3)\). More generally, when several terms are added together, the term with the largest asymptotic growth rate determines the Big-O bound. The lower-growth terms are still present in the original function, but they do not change the asymptotic upper bound.

Example 3

Since we have previously shown that \(5n^2-3n+20=O(n^2)\) and that \(3n^3-2n^2+13n-15=O(n^3)\), we know that \((5n^2-3n+20) +(3n^3-2n^2+13n-15)=O(n^2+n^3)=O(n^3)\).

Theorem 4: Products

If \(f_{1}(n) = O(g_{1}(n))\) and \(f_{2}(n) = O(g_{2}(n))\), then \[f_{1}(n)f_{2}(n) = O(g_{1}(n)g_{2}(n)).\] Similarly for \(\Theta\) and \(\Omega\).

Example 4

Since we have previously shown that \(5n^2-3n+20=O(n^2)\) and that \(3n^3-2n^2+13n-15=O(n^3)\), we know that \((5n^2-3n+20)(3n^3-2n^2+13n-15)=O(n^2n^3)=O(n^5)\). Notice that we could arrive at this same conclusion by multiplying the two polynomials and taking the highest term. However, this would require a lot more work than is necessary.

The next theorem essentially says that if \(g(n)\) is an upper bound on \(f(n)\), then \(f(n)\) is a lower bound on \(g(n)\). This makes perfect sense if you think about it.

Theorem 5: Big-O/Big-Omega Duality

\(f(n)=O(g(n))\) iff \(g(n)=\Omega(f(n))\).

There are many other properties of asymptotic notation, but these are the ones we will use most often. The main point is not to memorize a long list of rules. Instead, make sure you understand why these properties follow from the definitions. When in doubt, return to the definitions of \(O\), \(\Omega\), and \(\Theta\), choose appropriate constants, and check that the required inequality holds for all sufficiently large \(n\).

It turns out that \(\Theta\) defines an equivalence relation on the set of functions from the positive integers \(\mathbb{Z}^{+}\) to the positive integers \(\mathbb{Z}^{+}\). That is, it defines a partition on these functions, with two functions being in the same partition (or the same equivalence class) if and only if they have the same growth rate. Thus \(g(n)=\Theta(f(n))\), and \(R\) is symmetric.

Transitive: If \(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\), then there exist positive constants \(c_1\), \(c_2\), and \(n_0\) such that \[ c_1 g(n)\leq f(n) \leq c_2 g(n) \mbox{ for all } n\geq n_0. \] Similarly, if \(g(n)=\Theta(h(n))\), then there exist positive constants \(c_3\), \(c_4\), and \(n_1\) such that

\[ c_3 h(n)\leq g(n) \leq c_4 h(n) \mbox{ for all } n\geq n_1. \]

Then \[ f(n)\geq c_1\, g(n) \geq c_1c_3\, h(n) \mbox{ for all } n\geq \max\{n_0,n_1\}, \] and

\[ f(n)\leq c_2\, g(n) \leq c_2c_4\, h(n) \mbox{ for all } n\geq \max\{n_0,n_1\}. \]

Thus,

\[ c_1c_3 h(n) \leq f(n) \leq c_2c_4 h(n) \mbox{ for all } n\geq \max\{n_0,n_1\}. \]

Since \(c_1c_3\) and \(c_2c_4\) are both positive constants, \(f(n)=\Theta(h(n))\) by the definition of \(\Theta\), so \(R\) is transitive.

Reading Comprehension Questions

  1. Comparison Analogy: In what way is \(O\) similar to \(\leq\), \(\Omega\) similar to \(\geq\), and \(\Theta\) similar to \(=\)?
  2. Limits of the Analogy: Why is the analogy between asymptotic notation and ordinary comparison symbols useful but not exact?
  3. Transitivity: What does the transitive property of Big-O say? State it using functions \(f\), \(g\), and \(h\).
  4. Scaling: If \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) and \(k>0\) is a constant, what can we conclude about \(k f(n)\)? Why does multiplying by \(k\) not change the asymptotic upper bound?
  5. Sums: If \(f_1(n)=O(g_1(n))\) and \(f_2(n)=O(g_2(n))\), what upper bound does Theorem 3 give for \(f_1(n)+f_2(n)\)?
  6. Dominant Terms: Why is \(O(n^2+n^3)\) usually written as \(O(n^3)\)?
  7. Products: If \(f_1(n)=O(g_1(n))\) and \(f_2(n)=O(g_2(n))\), what does the product theorem say about \(f_1(n)f_2(n)\)?
  8. Big-O/Big-Omega Duality: What does it mean to say that \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) iff \(g(n)=\Omega(f(n))\)?
  9. Equivalence Classes: Why do \(n^2\), \(3n^2-4n+4\), \(n^2+\log n\), and \(3n^2+n+1\) belong to the same \(\Theta\)-equivalence class?
  10. Proof Strategy: In several proofs, why do we choose \(n_0=\max\{n_1,n_2\}\)?
  1. \(O\) behaves like an asymptotic upper-bound relation, so it is similar to \(\leq\). \(\Omega\) behaves like an asymptotic lower-bound relation, so it is similar to \(\geq\). \(\Theta\) means two functions have the same asymptotic growth rate, so it is similar to \(=\).
  2. The analogy is useful because many familiar properties, such as transitivity, have asymptotic versions. However, it is not exact because asymptotic notation ignores constant factors and lower-order terms, while ordinary inequalities do not.
  3. The transitive property of Big-O says that if \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) and \(g(n)=O(h(n))\), then \(f(n)=O(h(n))\).
  4. We can conclude that \(k f(n)=O(g(n))\). Multiplying by a positive constant only changes the constant in the Big-O definition. If \(f(n)\leq c g(n)\), then \(k f(n)\leq kc g(n)\), and \(kc\) is still a positive constant.
  5. Theorem 3 gives \[ f_1(n)+f_2(n)=O(g_1(n)+g_2(n)). \] Since \(g_1(n)+g_2(n)=O(\max\{g_1(n),g_2(n)\})\), this is also commonly written as \[ f_1(n)+f_2(n)=O(\max\{g_1(n),g_2(n)\}). \]
  6. \(n^3\) grows faster than \(n^2\), so the \(n^3\) term determines the asymptotic upper bound. The \(n^2\) term is still part of the expression, but it does not change the Big-O growth rate.
  7. The product theorem says that \[ f_1(n)f_2(n)=O(g_1(n)g_2(n)). \]
  8. It means that saying \(g\) is an asymptotic upper bound for \(f\) is equivalent to saying \(f\) is an asymptotic lower bound for \(g\). The two statements express the same relationship from opposite directions.
  9. They all have quadratic growth. Constant factors and lower-order terms do not change the \(\Theta\)-class, so each function is \(\Theta(n^2)\).
  10. We choose \(n_0=\max\{n_1,n_2\}\) because the two inequalities may start being true at different values of \(n\). The first inequality is guaranteed for all \(n\geq n_1\), and the second is guaranteed for all \(n\geq n_2\). By choosing the larger of \(n_1\) and \(n_2\), we make sure that both inequalities are true for every \(n\geq n_0\). This lets us combine the two bounds in a single proof.
