Master Theorem

Master Theorem

The Master Theorem is a useful shortcut for solving recurrence relations that come from many divide-and-conquer algorithms. We will omit the proof, but we will use the theorem to quickly classify the growth rate of recurrences with a very specific form.

Theorem 1: Master Theorem

Let \(T(n)\) be a monotonically increasing function satisfying

\[ \begin{aligned} T(n)&=aT(n/b)+f(n)\\ T(1)&=c \end{aligned} \]

where \(a\geq 1\), \(b>1\), and \(c>0\). If \(f(n)=\Theta(n^d)\), where \(d\geq 0\), then

\[ T(n)=\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \Theta(n^d)&\text{if } a<b^d\\ \Theta(n^d\log n)&\text{if } a=b^d\\ \Theta(n^{\log_b{a}})&\text{if } a>b^d \end{array} \right. \]

In practice, the main work is identifying \(a\), \(b\), and \(d\), then comparing \(a\) with \(b^d\).

Example 1

Use the Master Theorem to solve the recurrence

\[ T(n) = 4T(n/2) + n, \qquad T(1)=1. \]

Solution

We have \(a=4\), \(b=2\), and \(d = 1\). Since \(4>2^1\), the third case of the Master Theorem applies. Therefore,

\[ T(n)=\Theta(n^{\log_2{4}})=\Theta(n^2). \]

Example 2

Use the Master Theorem to solve the recurrence

\[ T(n) = 4T(n/2) + n^{2}, \qquad T(1)=1. \]

Solution

We have \(a=4\), \(b=2\), and \(d=2\). Since \(4=2^2\), the second case of the Master Theorem applies. Therefore,

\[ T(n)=\Theta(n^2\log n). \]

Example 3

Use the Master Theorem to solve the recurrence

\[ T(n) = 4T(n/2) + n^{3}, \qquad T(1)=1. \]

Solution

Here, \(a=4\), \(b=2\), and \(d=3\). Since \(4<2^3\), the first case of the Master Theorem applies. Therefore,

\[ T(n)=\Theta(n^3). \]

That was quick. The ease of use of the Master Theorem comes with two costs. First, it gives an asymptotic bound, not an exact closed form. For algorithm analysis, that is usually what we want. If you need an exact formula, the Master Theorem will not provide it. Second, it only applies to recurrences with the exact form \(T(n)=aT(n/b)+f(n)\). It does not apply directly to similar-looking recurrences such as \(T(n)=T(n/b)+T(n/c)+f(n)\).

Exercise 1

Use the Master Theorem to solve the recurrence

\[ T(n) = 2T(n/2) + 1, \qquad T(1)=1. \]

Here, \(a=2\), \(b=2\), and \(d=0\). We use \(d=0\) because \(1=1\cdot n^0\). Since \(2>2^0\), the third case of the Master Theorem applies. Therefore,

\[ T(n)=\Theta(n^{\log_2 2})=\Theta(n). \]

Example 4

Let's redo one we solved earlier with iteration. Use the Master Theorem to solve the recurrence

\[ R(n) = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1&\mbox{when } n = 1\\ 2 R(n/2) + n/2 & \mbox{otherwise} \end{array} \right. \]

Solution

Here, we have \(a=2\), \(b=2\), and \(d=1\), since \(n/2=\Theta(n)\). Since \(2=2^1\), the second case of the Master Theorem applies. Therefore,

\[ R(n)=\Theta(n\log n). \]

This is consistent with the exact solution from the iteration method:

\[ R(n)=n+(\log_2 n)n/2=\Theta(n\log n). \]

Exercise 2

Use the Master Theorem to solve the recurrence

\[ T(n)=7T(n/2)+15n^2/4, \qquad T(1)=1. \]

We have \(a=7\), \(b=2\), and \(d=2\), since \(15n^2/4=\Theta(n^2)\). Since \(7>2^2\), the third case of the Master Theorem applies. Therefore,

\[ T(n)=\Theta(n^{\log_{2} 7}). \]

Since \(\log_2 7\approx 2.807\), this is a little larger than quadratic but smaller than cubic.

Question 1

In the solution to the previous exercise, we could say that

\(T(n)=\Theta(n^{\log_{2} 7})\), and \(\log_2 7\approx 2.807\).

Why should we not replace this with \(T(n)=\Theta(n^{2.8})\)?

Because \(2.8\) is only an approximation. The functions \(n^{\log_2 7}\) and \(n^{2.8}\) do not have exactly the same growth rate, so \(\Theta(n^{\log_2 7})\neq \Theta(n^{2.8})\). We can use the decimal approximation for intuition, but the exact exponent gives the tight asymptotic bound.

Exercise 3

Use the Master Theorem to solve the recurrence for the complexity of binary search:

\[ T(n)=T(n/2)+1, \qquad T(1)=1. \]

Here we have \(a=1\), \(b=2\), and \(d=0\). Since \(1=2^0\), the second case of the Master Theorem applies. Therefore,

\[ T(n)=\Theta(n^0\log n)=\Theta(\log n). \]

This is consistent with the exact solution \(T(n)=\log_2 n+1\) for powers of \(2\).

Reading Comprehension Questions

  1. Form of the Recurrence: What form must a recurrence have in order to use the Master Theorem as stated on this page?
  2. Identifying the Parameters: In \(T(n)=5T(n/3)+n^2\), what are \(a\), \(b\), and \(d\)?
  3. Comparing Quantities: After identifying \(a\), \(b\), and \(d\), what comparison determines which case of the theorem applies?
  4. Exact Versus Asymptotic: Why does the Master Theorem not usually give the same kind of answer as the iteration method?
  5. Constant Work: Why is \(d=0\) when \(f(n)\) is a positive constant?
  6. Middle Case: What extra factor appears in the answer when \(a=b^d\)?
  7. Approximate Exponents: Why is \(\Theta(n^{\log_2 7})\) not the same as \(\Theta(n^{2.8})\)?
  8. When Not to Use It: Why does the theorem on this page not apply directly to \(T(n)=T(n/2)+T(n/3)+n\)?
  1. Answer: It must have the form \(T(n)=aT(n/b)+f(n)\), with one recursive term, a constant number \(a\) of subproblems, each of size \(n/b\), and non-recursive work \(f(n)=\Theta(n^d)\).
  2. Answer: We have \(a=5\), \(b=3\), and \(d=2\), since the non-recursive term is \(n^2\).
  3. Answer: Compare \(a\) with \(b^d\). The three cases are \(a<b^d\), \(a=b^d\), and \(a>b^d\).
  4. Answer: The Master Theorem gives an asymptotic bound, such as \(\Theta(n\log n)\), rather than an exact closed form. The iteration method can sometimes produce an exact formula.
  5. Answer: A positive constant can be written as \(c\cdot n^0\), and \(n^0=1\). Thus constant work is \(\Theta(n^0)\).
  6. Answer: The answer gains a logarithmic factor. If \(a=b^d\), then \(T(n)=\Theta(n^d\log n)\).
  7. Answer: The value \(2.8\) is only an approximation to \(\log_2 7\). Since the exponents are not exactly equal, the functions do not have exactly the same asymptotic growth rate.
  8. Answer: It has two recursive terms with different input sizes, \(n/2\) and \(n/3\). The theorem on this page applies to recurrences with one recursive term of the form \(aT(n/b)\).

    1. \(T(n)=3T(n/2)+n\)
    2. \(T(n)=2T(n/4)+n^{1/2}\)
    3. \(T(n)=8T(n/2)+n^3\)
    4. \(T(n)=5T(n/5)+7n\)
  2. Case Sorting: Sort the following recurrences into the first, second, and third cases of the Master Theorem. Briefly justify each placement.
    1. \(T(n)=2T(n/2)+n^2\)
    2. \(T(n)=4T(n/2)+n^2\)
    3. \(T(n)=8T(n/2)+n^2\)
    4. \(T(n)=9T(n/3)+n^2\)
  3. Same \(a\) and \(b\), Different Work: Compare the three recurrences \[ T(n)=4T(n/2)+n,\qquad T(n)=4T(n/2)+n^2,\qquad T(n)=4T(n/2)+n^3. \] Explain why changing only the non-recursive work changes which case applies.
  4. Does the Theorem Apply? For each recurrence below, decide whether the Master Theorem on this page applies directly. If it does not, identify which part of the required form is missing.
    1. \(T(n)=2T(n/2)+n\)
    2. \(T(n)=T(n/2)+T(n/3)+n\)
    3. \(T(n)=3T(n-1)+n\)
    4. \(T(n)=2T(n/2)+n\log n\)
  5. Check a Claimed Solution: A student claims that \(T(n)=2T(n/2)+n\) has solution \(\Theta(n)\) because \(f(n)=n\). Check this claim using the Master Theorem and correct it if necessary.
  6. Build Your Own: Write one recurrence of the form \(T(n)=aT(n/b)+n^d\) for each case of the Master Theorem. For each one, state the resulting asymptotic bound.
