Mixed Practice

In the previous sections, each recurrence was usually placed near the method used to solve it. That made it easier to focus on one technique at a time. In practice, however, recurrence relations do not arrive with labels. Before solving one, we often have to decide which method is appropriate.

This page gives mixed practice with the recurrence-solving methods from this part of the book. For each recurrence, first decide which method looks promising. Then solve the recurrence, prove a proposed bound, or explain why a particular method does not apply.

Strategy Reminder

  • Substitution: Useful when you already have a good guess and need to prove it. It is especially helpful for proving asymptotic bounds.
  • Iteration: Useful when repeated expansion creates a visible pattern or sum. It often gives more detail than an asymptotic theorem.
  • Master Theorem: Useful for divide-and-conquer recurrences of the form \(T(n)=aT(n/b)+f(n)\), especially when \(f(n)\) is a simple polynomial.
  • Linear recurrence techniques: Useful for recurrences such as \(x_n=ax_{n-1}+bx_{n-2}\), especially when an exact closed form is requested.

We include one example before the activities and homework problems. The example shows how the same recurrence can be approached in more than one way.

Example 1

Solve or tightly bound the recurrence \[ T(n)=2T(n/2)+n,\qquad T(1)=1. \] Assume \(n\) is a power of \(2\). We will solve the same recurrence in three different ways: iteration, the Master Theorem, and substitution.

Solution 1: Iteration

First, expand the recurrence several times:

\[ \begin{aligned} T(n)&=2T(n/2)+n\\ &=2\left(2T(n/4)+n/2\right)+n\\ &=2^2T(n/4)+n+n\\ &=2^2\left(2T(n/8)+n/4\right)+2n\\ &=2^3T(n/8)+3n\\ &\vdots\\ &=2^kT(n/2^k)+kn. \end{aligned} \]

To reach the base case, we need \[ n/2^k=1. \] Thus \(2^k=n\), so \(k=\log_2 n\). Substituting this value of \(k\), we get

\[ \begin{aligned} T(n)&=2^{\log_2 n}T(n/2^{\log_2 n})+n\log_2 n\\ &=nT(1)+n\log_2 n\\ &=n+n\log_2 n. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, \[ T(n)=n+n\log_2 n=\Theta(n\log n). \]

Solution 2: Master Theorem

The recurrence has the form \[ T(n)=aT(n/b)+f(n). \] Here, \[ a=2,\qquad b=2,\qquad f(n)=n. \] Since \(f(n)=\Theta(n^1)\), we have \(d=1\). Now compare \(a\) with \(b^d\):

\[ a=2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad b^d=2^1=2. \]

Since \(a=b^d\), the second case of the Master Theorem applies. Therefore,

\[ T(n)=\Theta(n^d\log n)=\Theta(n\log n). \]

This agrees with the result from iteration.

Solution 3: Substitution

From the first solution, we found the closed form \[ T(n)=n+n\log_2 n. \] We can verify this formula by substitution. Assume \(n\) is a power of \(2\). The base case works because

\[ T(1)=1+1\log_2 1=1. \]

Now suppose the formula holds for \(n/2\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} T(n)&=2T(n/2)+n\\ &=2\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n}{2}\log_2(n/2)\right)+n\\ &=n+n\log_2(n/2)+n\\ &=n+n(\log_2 n-1)+n\\ &=n+n\log_2 n. \end{aligned} \]

Thus the closed form is correct. In particular, \[ T(n)=n+n\log_2 n=\Theta(n\log n). \]

The three methods give consistent information. Iteration gives an exact closed form, the Master Theorem gives the asymptotic bound quickly, and substitution verifies the claimed closed form.

Reading Comprehension Questions

  1. Choosing a Method: Why is method selection part of solving recurrence relations?
  2. Master Theorem Form: What form must a recurrence have before the Master Theorem can be applied directly?
  3. Iteration Pattern: Why is iteration often useful for recurrences such as \(T(n)=T(n-1)+n\) or \(T(n)=T(n/2)+n\)?
  4. Substitution Role: If someone claims that \(T(n)=O(n^2)\), what can the substitution method help you do?
  5. Exact Form or Asymptotic Bound: Why might the Master Theorem be less useful if you need an exact closed form?
  6. Linear Recurrence Recognition: Why is \(x_n=3x_{n-1}-2x_{n-2}\) a better candidate for the linear recurrence method than for the Master Theorem?
  7. Multiple Methods: Give an example of a recurrence that can be handled by both iteration and the Master Theorem.
  8. Method Failure: Why does the Master Theorem not directly apply to \(T(n)=T(n-1)+n\)?
  9. Overlapping Methods: Can a recurrence be solved using both the Master Theorem and the linear recurrence techniques from the previous page? Explain why or why not.
  1. Answer: Different recurrences have different structures. Some match the Master Theorem, some expand cleanly by iteration, some require a proof by substitution, and some are linear recurrence relations with characteristic equations. Choosing the method is part of recognizing the structure.
  2. Answer: It must have the form \(T(n)=aT(n/b)+f(n)\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are constants with \(a\geq 1\) and \(b>1\). In the version used here, \(f(n)\) should be comparable to a polynomial such as \(n^d\).
  3. Answer: These recurrences usually produce a simple sequence of non-recursive terms when expanded. For example, \(T(n)=T(n-1)+n\) produces terms such as \(n+(n-1)+(n-2)+\cdots\), while \(T(n)=T(n/2)+n\) produces terms such as \(n+n/2+n/4+\cdots\).
  4. Answer: The substitution method can be used to prove the claimed bound. You assume the bound holds for smaller inputs, substitute that assumption into the recurrence, and then show that the desired bound follows for input size \(n\).
  5. Answer: The Master Theorem gives an asymptotic bound such as \(\Theta(n\log n)\). It usually does not give the exact constants or lower-order terms in the closed form.
  6. Answer: The recurrence is written in terms of previous sequence values, \(x_{n-1}\) and \(x_{n-2}\), rather than a divided input size such as \(T(n/2)\). It fits the form of a second-order homogeneous linear recurrence.
  7. Answer: One example is \(T(n)=2T(n/2)+n\). The Master Theorem gives \(\Theta(n\log n)\), and iteration shows that each of the \(\log_2 n\) levels contributes \(n\).
  8. Answer: The recursive argument is \(n-1\), not \(n/b\) for a constant \(b>1\). Therefore it is not in the divide-and-conquer form required by the Master Theorem.

The Master Theorem applies to divide-and-conquer recurrences of the form \[ T(n)=aT(n/b)+f(n), \] where the recursive input is divided by a constant. Linear recurrence techniques apply to recurrences such as \[ x_n=c_1x_{n-1}+c_2x_{n-2}+\cdots+c_kx_{n-k}+f(n), \] where the recursive terms use previous indices. Thus \(T(n)=2T(n/2)+n\) fits the Master Theorem form, while \(x_n=3x_{n-1}+2\) fits the linear recurrence form. The two methods usually apply to different families of recurrences.
