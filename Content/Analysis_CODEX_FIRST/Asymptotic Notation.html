Asymptotic Notation

Overview

Algorithm analysis often turns a program into a function. The input size is \(n\), and the function describes how much work the algorithm does on inputs of that size. Asymptotic notation gives us a careful language for comparing those functions without getting trapped by machine details, constant factors, or the behavior of very small inputs.

This page introduces the main notations: Big-O, Big-Omega, Big-Theta, and little-o. The next page focuses on proof techniques for these notations.

Why Asymptotic Notation?

Suppose one implementation performs \(5n+20\) basic operations and another performs \(3n^2+10n+7\). Exact counts may matter for small inputs, but for large inputs the dominant question is how the work grows. Linear growth and quadratic growth are different enough that the quadratic algorithm will eventually require much more work, even if it has a smaller constant for small \(n\).

Asymptotic notation describes what happens as \(n\) grows without bound. We intentionally ignore a finite number of small inputs. This is why the definitions below include a threshold \(n_0\): after \(n\) is large enough, the comparison must hold.

Big-O: Upper Bounds

Definition

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be nonnegative functions. We say that \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) if there are positive constants \(c\) and \(n_0\) such that \[ f(n) \leq c g(n) \quad \text{for all } n \geq n_0. \]

If \(f(n)=O(g(n))\), then \(f\) grows no faster than \(g\). Equivalently, \(g\) is an asymptotic upper bound on \(f\).

The constant \(c\) lets us ignore constant factors. The threshold \(n_0\) lets us ignore a finite prefix of small values. For example, \(100n=O(n^2)\), even though \(100n\) is larger than \(n^2\) for several small values of \(n\). Eventually \(n^2\) grows large enough to dominate the constant factor.

Be careful with the equals sign. The statement \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) is read as "\(f(n)\) is Big-O of \(g(n)\)." It is not a symmetric equality. The set notation \(f(n) \in O(g(n))\) is more precise, but the equals-style notation is common.

Example: A Simple Upper Bound

Show that \(n^2+n=O(n^3)\).

For \(n \geq 1\), we have \(n \leq n^3\) and \(n^2 \leq n^3\). Therefore \[ n^2+n \leq n^3+n^3 = 2n^3. \] This proves \(n^2+n=O(n^3)\), using \(c=2\) and \(n_0=1\).

Lower and Tight Bounds

Big-Omega

\(f(n)=\Omega(g(n))\) if there are positive constants \(c\) and \(n_0\) such that \[ c g(n) \leq f(n) \quad \text{for all } n \geq n_0. \]

\(g\) is an asymptotic lower bound on \(f\).

Big-Theta

\(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\) if there are positive constants \(c_1\), \(c_2\), and \(n_0\) such that \[ c_1g(n) \leq f(n) \leq c_2g(n) \quad \text{for all } n \geq n_0. \]

\(g\) is an asymptotically tight bound on \(f\).

A useful theorem connects these ideas: \[ f(n)=\Theta(g(n)) \quad \text{if and only if} \quad f(n)=O(g(n)) \text{ and } f(n)=\Omega(g(n)). \] In practice, many \(\Theta\)-proofs are split into an upper-bound proof and a lower-bound proof.

Example: A Tight Bound

Show that \(n^2+5n+7=\Theta(n^2)\).

For \(n\geq 1\), \[ n^2+5n+7 \leq n^2+5n^2+7n^2=13n^2. \] Also, for \(n\geq 0\), \(n^2 \leq n^2+5n+7\). Combining the two inequalities under the stronger assumption \(n\geq 1\), we get \[ n^2 \leq n^2+5n+7 \leq 13n^2. \] Therefore \(n^2+5n+7=\Theta(n^2)\).

Little-o

Big-O says "no faster than." Sometimes we want to say "strictly slower than." That is what little-o notation does.

Definition

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be nonnegative functions, with \(g(n)\) eventually nonzero. We say \(f(n)=o(g(n))\) if \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=0. \]

If \(f(n)=o(g(n))\), then \(f\) grows asymptotically slower than \(g\).

For example, \(3n+2=o(n^2)\), but \(3n+2\neq o(n)\). Similarly, \(n^2+n+4=o(n^3)\), but \(n^2+n+4\neq o(n^2)\). The first pair has different growth rates; the second pair has the same growth rate.

There is also a little-omega notation, \(\omega\), for "strictly faster than." We will use it less often, but it is the natural counterpart to little-o.

Comparison Summary

Notation Informal meaning Inequality analogy What it tells you
\(f(n)=O(g(n))\) \(f\) grows no faster than \(g\) \(\leq\) Upper bound only; may or may not be tight
\(f(n)=\Omega(g(n))\) \(f\) grows no slower than \(g\) \(\geq\) Lower bound only; may or may not be tight
\(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\) \(f\) and \(g\) grow at the same rate \(=\) Tight asymptotic bound
\(f(n)=o(g(n))\) \(f\) grows strictly slower than \(g\) \(<\) Strict upper relationship

Quick check: If \(f(n)=O(g(n))\), must \(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\)?

No. For example, \(n=O(n^2)\), but \(n\neq\Theta(n^2)\). Big-O gives an upper bound; it does not promise that the bound is tight.

Reading Comprehension Questions

  1. In words, what does \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) mean?
  2. What extra information does \(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\) give compared with \(f(n)=O(g(n))\)?
  3. Why does the definition of Big-O include both a constant \(c\) and a threshold \(n_0\)?
  4. Explain the difference between \(f(n)=o(g(n))\) and \(f(n)=O(g(n))\).
  5. If \(f(n)=\Theta(g(n))\), is it possible that \(f(n)=o(g(n))\)? Explain.
  1. Answer: \(f(n)=O(g(n))\) means that \(f\) grows no faster than \(g\), up to a constant factor, once \(n\) is large enough.
  2. Answer: \(\Theta\) gives a tight bound. It says the two functions grow at the same rate, while Big-O only gives an upper bound.
  3. Answer: The constant handles constant-factor differences, and \(n_0\) lets the comparison ignore small inputs where the long-term pattern may not yet be visible.
  4. Answer: Little-o means strictly slower growth. Big-O means no faster growth, so it includes both slower growth and same-rate growth.
  5. Answer: No. \(\Theta\) says same rate; little-o says strictly slower. Both cannot be true for the same pair of functions.

In-Class Activities

  1. Classify the statement: For each claim \(f=O(g)\), \(f=\Omega(g)\), \(f=\Theta(g)\), or \(f=o(g)\), write one sentence explaining what it says in ordinary language.
  2. Find counterexamples: Give simple functions \(f\) and \(g\) where \(f=O(g)\) but \(f\neq\Theta(g)\).
  3. Match the graph: Sketch a possible graph for an upper bound, a lower bound, and a tight bound. Label \(f(n)\), \(g(n)\), \(c\), and \(n_0\).

Homework Problems

  1. Prove directly from the definition that \(3n^3-4n^2+13n=O(n^3)\).
  2. Prove that \(5n^2-7n=\Theta(n^2)\).
  3. Give an example where \(f(n)=\Omega(g(n))\) but \(f(n)\neq\Theta(g(n))\).
  4. For each statement, decide whether it is true or false and justify briefly: \(f=O(g)\Rightarrow g=O(f)\), \(f=\Theta(g)\Rightarrow f=O(g)\), and \(f=o(g)\Rightarrow f=O(g)\).
