Common Growth Rates

Overview

After learning the definitions of asymptotic notation, the next step is recognizing the growth rates that occur most often in algorithm analysis. Some comparisons are intuitive after enough practice: logarithms grow slowly, polynomials grow faster than logarithms, exponentials eventually beat polynomials, and factorials grow even faster than many exponentials.

This page summarizes those comparisons and gives practice ranking functions by growth rate. It continues the mathematical part of the LaTeX chapter before the pages move into algorithm analysis itself.

Visual Comparisons

Graphs and tables can help build intuition, but they can also mislead. A function may look smaller on a graph simply because the plotted range is too small. For example, \(100n\) is larger than \(2^n\) for small \(n\), but \(2^n\) eventually becomes much larger.

Comparison Rules

The following relationships are the main facts to remember. They can all be proved using the limit theorem from the previous page, with l'Hopital's Rule needed for some logarithm comparisons.

Relationship Meaning Example
If \(a<b\), then \(n^a=o(n^b)\) Higher powers of \(n\) grow faster than lower powers. \(n^2=o(n^3)\)
If \(0<a<b\), then \(a^n=o(b^n)\) For exponentials, the larger base grows faster. \(2^n=o(5^n)\)
\(\log_a n=\Theta(\log_b n)\) Changing a logarithm base only changes a constant factor. \(\log_2 n=\Theta(\ln n)\)
\(\log^a n=o(n^b)\) for \(a,b>0\) Any fixed power of a logarithm grows slower than any positive power of \(n\). \(\log^{100} n=o(\sqrt n)\)
\(n^a=o(b^n)\) for \(a>0\), \(b>1\) Any fixed polynomial grows slower than any exponential with base greater than 1. \(n^{20}=o(1.01^n)\)

A Growth-Rate Chain

A useful ordering, from slower growth to faster growth, is:

\[ c \ll \log n \ll \log^2 n \ll \sqrt n \ll n \ll n\log n \ll n^{1.1} \ll n^2 \ll n^3 \ll 2^n \ll 3^n \ll n! \ll n^n. \]

The symbol \(\ll\) is another way to write little-o. So \(f(n)\ll g(n)\) means \(f(n)=o(g(n))\). You do not need to memorize only the symbols. It is better to understand the reason behind each jump: constants do not grow, logarithms grow slowly, powers of \(n\) outrun logarithms, exponentials outrun polynomials, and factorials count an enormous number of arrangements.

Worked Ranking Example

Rank the following functions in increasing order of growth:

\(n^n\), \(7\log_{10}n\), \(n^2+n+1\), \(7^n\), \(3n^2\), \(2^n\), \(n!\), \(\log_3 n\), \(7n\log_2 n\), \(n^3\), \(27n\), \(8675309\), and \(n^3+n^2\log_e n\).

\[ 8675309,\quad 7\log_{10}n \sim \log_3 n,\quad 27n,\quad 7n\log_2 n,\quad n^2+n+1 \sim 3n^2,\quad n^3 \sim n^3+n^2\log_e n,\quad 2^n,\quad 7^n,\quad n!,\quad n^n. \]

The \(\sim\) symbol here means "same growth rate." Constant multipliers do not change a growth rate, so \(7\log_{10}n\) and \(\log_3n\) are both logarithmic. Lower-order terms do not change a polynomial's growth rate, so \(n^3+n^2\log n\) is still \(\Theta(n^3)\), because \(n^2\log n=o(n^3)\).

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming graphs prove growth rates: A graph over a small range can hide the eventual behavior.
  • Treating exponential bases like logarithm bases: Logarithm bases differ only by constants, but exponential bases change the growth rate.
  • Forgetting that multiplication by a growing function increases growth: \(n\log n\) grows faster than \(n\), even though \(\log n\) grows slowly.
  • Dropping lower-order terms too early in a proof: It is fine in a final simplified answer, but a formal proof needs justification.

Reading Comprehension Questions

  1. Why does \(n\log n\) grow faster than \(cn\) for every constant \(c>0\)?
  2. Does saying "\(\log n\) grows slowly" mean \(f(n)\log n\) grows slower than \(f(n)\)? Explain.
  3. Why do \(\log_2 n\) and \(\log_{10} n\) have the same asymptotic growth rate?
  4. Which grows faster, \(n^{100}\) or \(1.01^n\)? What comparison rule justifies your answer?
  5. Why is \(n^3+n^2\log n=\Theta(n^3)\)?
  1. Answer: Both have a factor of \(n\), but \(n\log n\) has the additional growing factor \(\log n\). Constants do not grow.
  2. Answer: No. \(\log n\) grows slowly, but it still grows. Multiplying \(f(n)\) by \(\log n\) makes the product grow faster than \(f(n)\), assuming \(f(n)\) is positive.
  3. Answer: The change-of-base formula says \(\log_a n=\log_b n/\log_b a\). The divisor is a constant, and constant factors do not affect asymptotic growth.
  4. Answer: \(1.01^n\) eventually grows faster. Any exponential with base greater than 1 grows faster than any fixed polynomial.
  5. Answer: The \(n^3\) term dominates, because \(n^2\log n=o(n^3)\). The lower-order term does not change the tight bound.

In-Class Activities

  1. Sort the cards: Put cards labeled \(\log n\), \(n\), \(n\log n\), \(n^2\), \(2^n\), \(n!\), and \(n^n\) in increasing growth order. Then add \(\sqrt n\), \(\log^2 n\), and \(3^n\).
  2. Same or different: Decide whether each pair has the same growth rate: \(\log_2 n\) and \(\ln n\); \(2^n\) and \(3^n\); \(n^2+n\) and \(5n^2\); \(n\log n\) and \(n\).
  3. Graph skepticism: Sketch a small-\(n\) graph where \(100n\) is above \(2^n\), then explain why the graph does not prove \(100n\) grows faster.

Homework Problems

  1. Give a \(\Theta\)-bound for \(n^5+n^3+1900+n^7+21n+n^2\).
  2. Give a \(\Theta\)-bound for \(49\cdot 2^n+34\cdot 3^n\).
  3. Give a \(\Theta\)-bound for \(\log^{300}n+n^{0.000001}\).
  4. Rank the following in increasing order: \(x\), \(x^2\), \(2^x\), \(10000\), \(\log^{300}x\), \(x^5\), \(\log x\), \(x^{\log_2 3}\), \(x^{0.000001}\), \(3^x\), \(x\log x\), \(\log(x^{300})\), and \(\log(2^x)\).
