How to Use This Textbook

A Productive Reading Workflow

Treat each page as something to work through rather than something to read once and set aside. A productive sequence is:

  1. Identify the problem being solved or the operations being supported. Make sure you understand the central idea before studying implementation details.
  2. Trace a small example by hand. At each step, explain what changes, what remains true, and why the next step is valid.
  3. Use an interactive demo predictively: decide what should happen next, then advance the demo to check your reasoning.
  4. Study pseudocode and program code only after you understand the process they implement. Pay attention to how the code handles boundary cases.
  5. Answer the Reading Comprehension Questions before displaying the supplied answers, then correct and annotate your original responses.
  6. Complete selected activities or homework problems that require you to trace, explain, analyze, modify, test, or implement the idea yourself.

Test whether you can reproduce the important steps and explain the idea without looking at the page. By the end, you should be able to describe the goal, the main steps or operations, the important invariants, the reason the method works, its resource costs, and when it is appropriate. If you remain stuck after reviewing the definitions and examples, record a specific question and ask for help.

For the purpose, scope, organization, and development status of the project, see About DAIADS.

Background Assumptions

This material assumes that students have completed an introductory programming course. It does not attempt to teach a specific programming language, object-oriented programming, syntax for generics or templates, exception handling, unit testing, or basic programming constructs such as loops, conditionals, arrays, functions, and classes. Individual instructors may need to review some of these topics depending on the language used in their course and the background of their students.

The Analysis section and some of the Algorithms and Techniques sections also assume mathematical maturity typically developed in a discrete mathematics course.

When implementation details depend heavily on a language, the text tries to emphasize the underlying data-structure or algorithmic idea first. Language-specific details can be handled in class, lab, or supplemental examples.

Choosing a Path Through the Site

The textbook is modular. A course or independent reader does not need to follow one fixed sequence through the site. Some pages are intended as core readings, while others can be used as references, review material, or supplements when a particular topic is needed.

Choose a Navigation View

Use the View menu at the top of the navigation sidebar to choose the organization that best matches your purpose. Changing views does not leave the page you are reading; it changes the table of contents shown beside it.

  • All: Grouped shows every page, organized by major area and topic. This is the best view for exploring related material and understanding how topics fit together.
  • All: Alphabetic also shows every page, but lists the pages within each major area alphabetically. Use it when you know a page title but are not sure which topic contains it.
  • Intro Data Structures provides a focused, numbered path through the material commonly used in an introductory data structures course.
  • Intro Algorithms provides a focused, numbered path for an introductory algorithms course.

The two course views intentionally omit material that is not part of their main reading sequence. You can still follow a link to an omitted page; the sidebar will identify it as a supplementary page that is not in the selected book's table of contents. Switch to either All view whenever you want to browse the complete collection.

Select a heading in the sidebar to open or close that part of the table of contents. The double-arrow buttons expand or collapse all headings at once, and the menu button in the page header hides or restores the entire sidebar when you want more reading space.

Introduction to Data Structures

Choose Intro Data Structures for a suggested course path. It begins with foundations, then covers searching, sorting, linear structures, trees, priority queues and heaps, sets and maps, hash tables, and graphs. Instructors can follow the numbered order or adapt it to the needs of their course.

Introduction to Algorithms

Choose Intro Algorithms for a suggested course path that emphasizes algorithm analysis, design techniques, and representative algorithms. Data structure pages can still be consulted when they are needed to understand an algorithm; pages outside the curated path remain available through links and the two All views.

Independent Study or Review

For a structured independent study, select the course view closest to your goals and follow its numbered sequence. For targeted review, use All: Grouped to explore a topic and its related pages, or All: Alphabetic to locate a known title quickly. The glossary, problem pages, data structure pages, and demos can be used as references whenever unfamiliar concepts or techniques arise.

Using Individual Pages

Section headings vary because different kinds of pages emphasize different questions. An algorithm page usually develops a problem, a strategy, an implementation, and an analysis. A data structure page more often focuses on supported operations, representations, invariants, and implementation tradeoffs. Not every page needs every section.

Headings such as Overview, Problem Solved, Design and Strategy, Core Operations, Implementation, and Analysis make up the main explanation and should generally be studied in order. Implementation code and interactive demos are discussed immediately after the list below; Reading Comprehension Questions, In-Class Activities, and Homework Problems have dedicated sections later on this page. The following list focuses on later or supplementary headings whose roles may be less obvious:

  • Sections titled Applications or Real-World Applications show where the idea appears in software, other algorithms, or practical systems.
  • A Connections and Comparisons section relates the topic to other algorithms, data structures, or representations and explains the important similarities and differences.
  • Sections titled Variations and Extensions or Variations/Improvements explore related forms of the idea, useful adaptations, and ways to improve the basic approach.
  • Common Pitfalls sections identify misconceptions, implementation mistakes, and edge cases that deserve special attention.
  • Summary or Key Takeaways sections gather the main ideas that you should retain after studying the page.
  • Related Links and Resources sections point to relevant material elsewhere on DAIADS or to carefully selected external references for further study.

Do not begin with the code. First make sure you understand what the algorithm or data structure is trying to accomplish and how it works. Code is much easier to understand once the underlying idea is clear.

When several programming-language implementations are provided, students will normally concentrate on the language being used in their course. The other versions can be useful for comparison and for separating the underlying algorithm from language-specific details.

Interactive demos are most useful when used predictively. Before advancing a demo, decide what you think the algorithm should do next. Then use the demo to check your prediction. Simply clicking through the steps without making predictions turns an interactive tool into another form of passive reading.

Using Artificial Intelligence

Generative AI can be useful for asking for another explanation, requesting a hint, checking an implementation, generating another practice example, or discussing why a particular solution is incorrect. It is much less useful educationally when it performs the reasoning that the student was supposed to practice.

In particular, having an AI system generate answers to the Reading Comprehension Questions defeats much of their purpose. Attempt the questions yourself first. If AI tools are permitted in your course, they are generally more useful after that attempt as a source of feedback, hints, or alternative explanations.

Individual instructors may establish more specific policies about acceptable uses of AI for reading assignments, programming work, and homework problems.

Reading Comprehension Questions

Reading Comprehension Questions appear on many pages. They are intended as a final check that you understand the important ideas from the page before moving on to more substantial problems.

Students should attempt these questions without displaying the supplied answers. After completing the questions, check the answers even when you believe your own answers are correct. The answers may clarify details or emphasize points that are easy to overlook.

When an answer is incorrect, it is useful to preserve the original answer and then correct it rather than simply erasing or deleting it. Keeping track of mistakes makes it easier to identify topics that need additional attention and provides a useful record when studying later.

If you cannot answer a reading question after reviewing the relevant material, record the question and ask about it in class. One purpose of these questions is to identify misunderstandings before they become problems on more difficult assignments.

Activities and Homework Problems

Most pages contain more in-class activities and homework problems than any one course should use. This is intentional. They are a collection of options rather than a prescribed assignment.

Instructors should select the activities and problems that best match their students, course goals, programming language, available class time, and teaching style. A useful selection will often mix several kinds of work: tracing algorithms, analyzing running time, comparing alternatives, modifying algorithms or data structures, implementing them, and using demos to investigate their behavior.

Students working independently should make similar choices rather than assuming that every activity and problem must be completed. Concentrate especially on problems that require a kind of reasoning that you find difficult or that reveal something about the algorithm that was not obvious from reading the explanation.

Suggestions for Instructors

The site is deliberately modular and contains more material than should be used in a single course. Instructors should select pages, examples, activities, and homework problems that fit their course rather than attempting to cover everything.

One effective approach is to assign pages before class and require students to answer the Reading Comprehension Questions. These assignments can be graded primarily for serious effort or completion rather than for correctness. Their purpose is to get students to engage with the material before class and to reveal what still needs discussion.

Requiring handwritten answers on paper is worth considering. Besides making mathematical notation, diagrams, traces, and small pieces of code convenient to write, paper submissions make it easier to emphasize the student's own reasoning at a time when automatically generated responses are increasingly easy to produce. Students can correct their work in a different color after checking the supplied answers, preserving both their original attempt and what they learned from it.

Reading questions can also help determine how class time is used. Rather than repeating material students have already read, begin with questions students found difficult, compare different answers, trace additional examples, and move into selected in-class activities. In this way, the reading provides the initial presentation of the material and class time is used for the parts that benefit most from interaction.

The in-class activities are also intentionally more numerous than can normally be completed. Select the activities that connect most directly with the goals of the course and with difficulties students are actually having. The same principle applies to homework problems: choose a reasonably sized set that provides variety rather than assigning every problem merely because it appears on the page.

In courses using one primary programming language, students will normally need to study only that implementation carefully. Implementations in the other languages can be used selectively to emphasize which parts of an algorithm are fundamental and which are consequences of a particular language.

Because the text is modular, individual pages can also be assigned for review, pre-class preparation, laboratory support, independent study, or supplemental reading alongside another textbook.
