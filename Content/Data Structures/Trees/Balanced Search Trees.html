Balanced Search Trees

Overview

A balanced search tree is a search tree that maintains additional structural rules to keep its height \(O(\log n)\), where \(n\) is the number of stored keys. Since searching, inserting, and removing keys require following paths through the tree, controlling the height guarantees that these operations remain efficient even as the collection changes.

A plain binary search tree preserves the ordering of its keys but places no restriction on its shape. An unfavorable sequence of insertions or removals can therefore produce a long, nearly linear tree. Balanced search trees prevent this degeneration by detecting when their structural rules have been violated and repairing the tree after an update. Different tree families use different balance rules and different repair operations.

AVL trees and red-black trees are balanced binary search trees. They retain the ordinary binary search tree structure and use rotations, together with height or color information, to control the shape of the tree. Multiway search trees, such as 2-3 trees and B-trees, allow nodes to contain multiple keys and have more than two children. They maintain balance through operations such as splitting, promotion, redistribution, and merging.

Balanced does not mean perfect: A balanced search tree does not necessarily have every level filled, nor must the two subtrees of every node have exactly the same height. The word balanced refers to the particular structural conditions imposed by the tree family. What these definitions have in common is that they prevent the height from growing faster than \(O(\log n)\).

Why Balance Matters

The running time of the main binary search tree operations depends on the height \(h\) of the tree. Search and insertion follow a single path from the root, and removal follows only a constant number of such paths. Therefore, search, insert, and remove are all \(O(h\)).

When a tree is balanced, its height is \(\Theta(\log n)\), so these operations take \(O(\log n)\) time. A plain binary search tree, however, can become degenerate, with each node having only one child. Such a tree has height \(n-1\), and its operations may take \(O(n)\) time. Thus, the same set of keys can support very different performance depending on the shape of the tree.

Searching for \(70\) visits only three nodes in the balanced tree but all seven nodes in the degenerate tree. As the number of keys grows, this difference becomes much more significant: logarithmic paths grow slowly, while linear paths grow in direct proportion to the number of stored keys.

Maintaining balance adds work to insertion and removal because an update may need to repair the tree's structure. These repairs are worthwhile because they preserve logarithmic height, preventing later searches and updates from degrading to linear time. Balanced search trees therefore trade a limited amount of additional update work for a strong guarantee on the performance of every operation.

What Does Balanced Mean?

There is no single balance rule shared by all balanced search trees. Instead, each tree family maintains its own balance invariant: a structural condition strong enough to guarantee that the tree's height remains \(O(\log n)\). Some invariants compare subtree heights, some constrain paths using additional labels, and others allow each node to store several keys and have several children. Here are some of the most common structures and the rules they have.

Structure General Balance Rule Primary Repair Operations
AVL tree At every node, the heights of the left and right subtrees differ by at most one. Single and double rotations
Red-black tree Each node has a color, and rules involving those colors limit the difference between the lengths of root-to-leaf paths. Recoloring and rotations
2-3 tree Each internal node has two or three children, and all leaves occur at the same depth. Splitting, promotion, redistribution, and merging
B-tree Each node stores a bounded number of keys and children, subject to minimum and maximum occupancy rules, and all leaves occur at the same depth. Splitting, promotion, redistribution, and merging

These rules produce trees with different shapes and require different repair procedures, but they serve the same purpose: no root-to-leaf path can become disproportionately long as the number of stored keys grows. As a result, search paths remain logarithmic even after an unfavorable sequence of insertions and removals.

Maintaining Balance During Updates

Searching normally does not change the structure of a search tree, so it cannot create a new balance violation. Insertion and removal are different: both modify the tree, and the modified portion may no longer satisfy the balance invariant. A balanced search tree must therefore check for violations after an update and repair them before the operation is complete.

Although the details vary among tree families, balanced-tree updates usually follow the same general pattern:

  1. Follow the search-tree ordering to locate the insertion or removal position.
  2. Perform the ordinary search-tree update.
  3. Determine whether the tree's balance invariant has been violated.
  4. Repair the violation using the operations appropriate for that tree family.
  5. Continue toward the root if the repair may have created or exposed another violation.

Balanced binary search trees usually repair their structure with rotations, which change a small collection of parent-child relationships while preserving the search tree property. AVL trees use height information to choose among single and double rotations. Red-black trees use colors to guide a combination of recoloring and rotations.

Multiway search trees use different repair operations because a node may contain several keys and have more than two children. A node that becomes too full may be split, with one of its keys promoted to its parent. During removal, keys may be redistributed between neighboring nodes, or underfull nodes may be merged. Despite these differences, the goal is the same: restore the tree's balance invariant while preserving the sorted order of its keys.

Rotations in Balanced Binary Search Trees

Purpose and Anatomy of a Rotation

A rotation is a local transformation that changes the shape of a binary search tree without changing the keys it stores or their sorted order. Rotations are used by balanced binary search trees to shorten one side of a subtree and lengthen the other.

A rotation changes only a small number of parent-child relationships. Most of the tree is unaffected, and the subtrees attached to the rotated nodes retain their internal structure. However, one of those subtrees may need to be transferred from one rotated node to the other.

  • A left rotation moves a right child above its parent.
  • A right rotation moves a left child above its parent.
  • The node that moves upward is sometimes called the pivot.
  • The root of the rotated subtree may change, but its connection to the rest of the tree is preserved.
A rotation is not a complete balancing algorithm: A rotation can improve, preserve, or worsen the balance of a tree. A particular balanced-tree algorithm must determine when a rotation is necessary, where it should occur, and which direction should be used.

Left and Right Rotations

Left and right rotations are mirror images of one another. The pseudocode below assumes that each node stores left, right, and parent references and that the tree stores a root reference.

Left Rotation

Consider a node x whose right child is y. A left rotation moves y into x's former position and makes x the left child of y. The left subtree of y, labeled \(B\), becomes the right subtree of x.

Left Rotation 
rotateLeft(x):
y = x.right

if y == null:
    return

x.right = y.left

if y.left != null:
    y.left.parent = x

y.parent = x.parent

if x.parent == null:
    root = y
else if x == x.parent.left:
    x.parent.left = y
else:
    x.parent.right = y

y.left = x
x.parent = y

The transferred subtree \(B\) must be placed between x and y. Its keys are greater than the key at x but less than the key at y, so it becomes the right subtree of x.

Right Rotation

A right rotation is symmetric. If y has a left child x, the rotation moves x into y's former position and makes y the right child of x. The right subtree of x, labeled \(B\), becomes the left subtree of y.

Right Rotation 
rotateRight(y):
x = y.left

if x == null:
    return

y.left = x.right

if x.right != null:
    x.right.parent = y

x.parent = y.parent

if y.parent == null:
    root = x
else if y == y.parent.left:
    y.parent.left = x
else:
    y.parent.right = x

x.right = y
y.parent = x

What Rotations Preserve

Rotation Preservation Theorem: A left or right rotation preserves the set of stored keys and their inorder sequence. Therefore, if the tree satisfies the binary search tree property before the rotation, it also satisfies the property afterward.

To see why, consider a left rotation involving nodes \(x\) and \(y\) and subtrees \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\). Because the original tree is a binary search tree, every key in these groups satisfies

\[ A < x < B < y < C. \]

Here, \(A < x\), for example, means that every key in subtree \(A\) is less than the key at \(x\). Before the rotation, an inorder traversal visits the keys in the order

\[ A,\ x,\ B,\ y,\ C. \]

After the rotation, \(y\) is above \(x\), but the inorder sequence is still

\[ A,\ x,\ B,\ y,\ C. \]

The rotation therefore changes the shape of the tree but not the relative order of its keys. The same argument, reflected left to right, applies to a right rotation.

How Rotations Change Depth and Height

A rotation affects depths only within the rotated subtree. Consider again a left rotation at x, with right child y and subtrees \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\).

  • y and every node in \(C\) move up by one level.
  • x and every node in \(A\) move down by one level.
  • Every node in \(B\) remains at the same depth.
  • Every node outside the rotated subtree remains at the same depth.
Rotation Locality Lemma: A single rotation changes the depth of each node by at most one. Consequently, the height of the rotated subtree changes by at most one.

The internal structures and heights of \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) do not change. Only their attachment points change. If a balanced tree stores height or other balance information in its nodes, the information at the two rotated nodes must be recomputed. Information stored at their ancestors may also need to be updated.

A rotation does not necessarily reduce the height of the entire tree. It changes height in a controlled local way, and the balancing algorithm chooses rotations that repair a particular violation.

Double Rotations

Sometimes a single rotation is sufficient to repair the shape of a subtree. In other cases, the path through the affected nodes changes direction, and two rotations are needed. A double rotation is not a separate primitive operation; it is a sequence of two left or right rotations.

Left-Right Rotation

A left-right rotation first performs a left rotation on the left child and then performs a right rotation on the original subtree root. 

rotateLeftRight(z):
    rotateLeft(z.left)
    rotateRight(z)

Right-Left Rotation

A right-left rotation first performs a right rotation on the right child and then performs a left rotation on the original subtree root. 

rotateRightLeft(z):
    rotateRight(z.right)
    rotateLeft(z)

The AVL-tree page will show how subtree heights determine whether an imbalance requires a single rotation or a double rotation. Red-black trees use the same rotation operations but select them according to different rules.

Cost of Rotations

A single rotation changes only a constant number of references, so it takes \(O(1)\) time. A double rotation consists of two single rotations and therefore also takes \(O(1)\) time. Updating balance information at the rotated nodes also takes constant time when that information can be computed from their children.

The complete repair process may take longer because the algorithm must first locate the violation and may need to continue checking ancestors after the rotation. Those costs depend on the particular balanced-tree structure and will be analyzed on the pages for AVL trees, red-black trees, and other balanced search trees.
